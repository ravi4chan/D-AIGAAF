# Governance Committees

## 1. Purpose

Governance committees provide structured oversight for consequential AI capabilities where decisions require cross-functional expertise, independent challenge, or formal escalation.

D-AIGAAF committees are governance mechanisms. They do not replace clearly assigned decision rights, operational command authority, technical ownership, or independent assurance.

The purpose of this module is to define a generic committee architecture that implementing organisations can adapt to their own legal, policy, organisational and operational structures.

---

## 2. Governance Philosophy

D-AIGAAF applies governance proportionate to:

- mission consequence;
- autonomy;
- operational criticality;
- environmental complexity;
- human control;
- security and loss-of-control risk;
- supply-chain dependency;
- uncertainty and evidence gaps.

Not every AI capability requires every committee.

Governance should be sufficient to make a sound decision without creating unnecessary bureaucracy.

---

## 3. Why Committees Are Needed

Individual decision rights identify who has authority to decide.

Committees provide a mechanism to:

- bring together different areas of expertise;
- challenge assumptions;
- review evidence;
- identify cross-functional risks;
- resolve disagreements;
- escalate consequential decisions;
- maintain organisational oversight;
- ensure that no single function controls the entire governance process.

A committee should not become a substitute for accountable decision-making.

---

## 4. Proposed Governance Architecture

D-AIGAAF proposes the following generic governance constructs:

1. Strategic AI Governance Committee
2. Defence AI Assurance / Review Board
3. Operational AI Authorisation Board
4. AI Security and Resilience Review
5. Data and Information Governance Review
6. Acquisition and Supply Chain Review
7. Incident and Emergency Review Group

These are proposed framework constructs. They are not intended to imply that identical bodies exist within any particular defence organisation.

An implementing organisation may combine, rename, expand or distribute these functions according to its own structure.

---

## 5. Strategic AI Governance Committee

### Purpose

Provides strategic oversight of AI governance, policy alignment, priorities and organisational risk.

### Typical Responsibilities

- establish strategic AI governance direction;
- review major AI programmes and priorities;
- ensure alignment with applicable law, policy and doctrine;
- oversee organisational AI risk posture;
- approve governance policies and principles;
- review significant capability risks;
- resolve issues escalated beyond lower governance levels;
- oversee organisational maturity and implementation.

### Typical Participants

Membership should represent relevant functions such as:

- strategic leadership;
- operational leadership;
- policy and legal;
- technology;
- AI security;
- assurance;
- acquisition;
- information governance;
- workforce and training.

The exact membership should be determined by the implementing organisation.

---

## 6. Defence AI Assurance / Review Board

### Purpose

Provides independent or appropriately separated review of assurance evidence before consequential operational decisions.

### Typical Responsibilities

- review assurance claims;
- examine TEVV evidence;
- assess evidence gaps;
- review known limitations and failure modes;
- examine adversarial and operational testing;
- challenge unsupported assurance conclusions;
- determine whether evidence is sufficient to support an assurance position;
- recommend additional testing or restrictions;
- identify conditions requiring operational monitoring.

The board should not automatically grant operational authority merely because assurance evidence is satisfactory.

**Assurance does not equal authorisation.**

---

## 7. Operational AI Authorisation Board

### Purpose

Reviews whether an AI capability should receive operational authorisation within a defined mission, environment, autonomy level and human-authority arrangement.

### Typical Responsibilities

- review the operational authorisation case;
- confirm applicable decision rights;
- review the operational envelope;
- examine residual risk;
- verify human authority arrangements;
- review known limitations and constraints;
- confirm fail-safe and controlled-degradation arrangements;
- establish authorisation conditions;
- define review and expiry requirements;
- recommend authorisation, restriction, suspension or revocation;
- identify reauthorisation triggers.

Final operational authority must remain with the person or authority legally and organisationally empowered to grant it.

A board must not create authority that it does not itself possess.

---

## 8. AI Security and Resilience Review

### Purpose

Provides specialist review of security, adversarial resilience, loss-of-control and system integrity risks.

### Typical Areas of Review

- adversarial attacks;
- model manipulation;
- data poisoning;
- prompt or input manipulation;
- model and system integrity;
- supply-chain compromise;
- unauthorised modification;
- cyber dependencies;
- communications degradation;
- loss of control;
- malicious or unintended behaviour;
- fail-safe effectiveness;
- recovery and resilience.

Security review should continue throughout the lifecycle rather than occur only before deployment.

---

## 9. Data and Information Governance Review

### Purpose

Ensures that data and information used by AI systems are governed appropriately for mission, security, integrity and operational context.

### Typical Areas of Review

- data provenance;
- data quality;
- data integrity;
- classification and handling requirements;
- access control;
- retention;
- data drift;
- training-data risks;
- operational data dependencies;
- uncertainty arising from incomplete or degraded information.

Poor information can produce an operationally dangerous AI output even when the underlying model is functioning as designed.

---

## 10. Acquisition and Supply Chain Review

### Purpose

Provides governance of AI-related suppliers, dependencies and provenance throughout acquisition and sustainment.

### Typical Areas of Review

- model provenance;
- developer and supplier identity;
- critical software dependencies;
- hardware dependencies;
- third-party models;
- update mechanisms;
- subcontractors;
- supplier assurance;
- intellectual-property and licensing constraints;
- sovereignty considerations;
- continuity of supply;
- ability to independently test and verify critical components.

Supplier assurance does not replace independent assurance.

---

## 11. Incident and Emergency Review Group

### Purpose

Provides a mechanism for rapid governance response following serious AI incidents, loss of control, unexpected behaviour or significant changes in operational risk.

### Typical Triggers

- safety-critical failure;
- unauthorised behaviour;
- loss of meaningful human control;
- unexpected autonomous action;
- significant security compromise;
- material model behaviour change;
- repeated operational failure;
- serious harm or near miss;
- evidence that invalidates a previous assurance claim.

### Responsibilities

- establish immediate facts;
- support protective action;
- assess whether operational authority should be restricted or suspended;
- initiate risk reassessment;
- determine required investigation;
- identify evidence gaps;
- recommend revalidation or reauthorisation;
- capture lessons for future assurance.

Emergency protective action may be taken under pre-authorised procedures where delay could create unacceptable harm.

---

## 12. Committee Composition

Committee composition should reflect the risk and consequence of the capability under review.

Relevant expertise may include:

- operational command;
- AI engineering;
- systems engineering;
- AI security;
- cybersecurity;
- data governance;
- TEVV;
- assurance;
- legal and policy;
- acquisition;
- safety;
- human factors;
- operational AI advisory expertise.

For high-consequence systems, committees should avoid relying exclusively on personnel involved in developing or owning the capability.

---

## 13. Chair and Quorum

Each committee should define:

- chair;
- deputy or alternate;
- required voting or decision members;
- specialist advisers;
- quorum;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- escalation authority;
- emergency activation arrangements.

Quorum should be proportionate to consequence.

For particularly consequential decisions, absence of a required independent or operational perspective should normally prevent a formal decision unless an explicitly authorised emergency procedure applies.

---

## 14. Decision Rights vs Committee Role

Committees may:

- review;
- challenge;
- advise;
- recommend;
- escalate;
- approve matters explicitly delegated to them.

Committees must not:

- assume command authority without delegation;
- convert technical approval into operational authority;
- replace an authorised risk-acceptance authority;
- allow AI systems to determine their own governance status;
- silently delegate decision rights;
- override law, policy or higher authority.

The authority of a committee must be explicitly defined.

---

## 15. Independence

Where assurance or review is intended to be independent, the governance structure should provide sufficient separation from:

- system development;
- commercial incentives;
- programme delivery pressure;
- operational performance incentives;
- supplier interests.

Independence should be assessed according to the purpose of the review and the consequence of the decision.

---

## 16. Conflict of Interest

Potential conflicts should be declared and managed.

Examples include:

- reviewing a system one personally developed;
- approving a capability against which one's performance is measured;
- commercial interests in a supplier;
- operational pressure to deploy despite unresolved evidence gaps;
- institutional incentives to classify a risk as acceptable.

Conflict does not automatically disqualify participation, but it should be visible and appropriately managed.

---

## 17. Disagreement and Dissent

Governance should preserve legitimate technical and operational dissent.

Where material disagreement exists:

1. record the disagreement;
2. identify the evidence supporting each position;
3. identify unresolved assumptions;
4. identify the decision authority;
5. escalate where required;
6. record the final decision and rationale.

A dissenting view should not disappear merely because a committee reaches a majority position.

---

## 18. Committee Escalation Model

A typical escalation pathway is:

**Working-Level Review → Specialist Review → Assurance Review → Operational Authorisation Review → Strategic Escalation**

Emergency matters may bypass normal sequencing where delay could create unacceptable harm.

Bypassing a normal review process does not remove the requirement to document the decision and conduct subsequent review.

---

## 19. Committee Interaction With the OAIA

The Operational AI Advisor (OAIA) provides an important bridge between operational command and AI/system expertise.

The OAIA may:

- explain AI capabilities and limitations;
- interpret technical evidence for operational decision-makers;
- identify operational implications of uncertainty;
- advise on whether observed behaviour is expected;
- recommend escalation;
- support incident assessment;
- identify when developer or system-owner input is required.

The OAIA does not automatically replace the operational commander or authorising authority.

**OAIA advises; authorised human authority decides.**

---

## 20. Committee Records

Each committee should maintain appropriate records of:

- agenda;
- participants;
- quorum;
- evidence reviewed;
- key assumptions;
- risks identified;
- disagreements and dissent;
- decisions;
- recommendations;
- conditions;
- escalation;
- actions assigned;
- review dates.

Significant decisions should be traceable through the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread.

---

## 21. Relationship With Core Records

Committee outputs should contribute to the relevant D-AIGAAF records.

| Governance Activity | Relevant Record |
|---|---|
| Capability governance | DAICR |
| Assurance review | DAAR |
| Operational authorisation | DAOA |
| Operational employment | Operational Record |
| Incident review | Incident Record |
| Change review | Change Record |
| Revalidation | Revalidation Record |
| Reauthorisation | Reauthorisation Record |

Committee minutes should not become the only evidence of a consequential governance decision.

---

## 22. Meeting Cadence and Triggers

Governance activity should be triggered by risk and lifecycle events rather than by meetings alone.

Possible triggers include:

- new capability;
- new mission;
- increased autonomy;
- material change;
- new operational environment;
- significant incident;
- evidence degradation;
- supplier change;
- model update;
- security concern;
- policy change;
- expiry of authorisation.

Routine reviews may also be scheduled for high-consequence capabilities.

---

## 23. Emergency Governance

Emergency governance should support rapid protective decisions.

Pre-authorised emergency procedures should define:

- who may act;
- what protective actions are permitted;
- conditions for immediate action;
- notification requirements;
- recording requirements;
- post-event review;
- conditions for restoring normal authority.

Emergency governance must not become a permanent bypass around normal assurance and authorisation.

---

## 24. Governance Proportionality

Governance should scale with consequence.

A low-consequence advisory application may require limited review.

A system capable of independently taking consequential actions may require:

- multiple specialist reviews;
- independent assurance;
- formal operational authorisation;
- senior risk acceptance;
- continuous monitoring;
- explicit fail-safe arrangements;
- recurring reauthorisation.

The objective is not maximum bureaucracy.

The objective is **appropriate governance for the consequences of failure**.

---

## 25. Governance Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- committee proliferation;
- unclear committee authority;
- duplicated reviews;
- governance delays;
- lack of operational representation;
- lack of technical expertise;
- conflicted reviewers;
- assurance-authorisation confusion;
- committee approval treated as command authority;
- undocumented dissent;
- emergency procedures becoming routine;
- committee decisions disconnected from operational records;
- governance continuing after material system change without reassessment.

---

## 26. Minimum Governance Requirements

For consequential AI capabilities, D-AIGAAF should require:

1. Explicit governance ownership.
2. Defined decision rights.
3. Proportionate cross-functional review.
4. Appropriate separation of development, assurance and authorisation.
5. Defined escalation routes.
6. Conflict-of-interest management.
7. Recorded decisions and dissent.
8. Emergency governance arrangements.
9. Traceability to DAICR, DAAR and DAOA where applicable.
10. Reassessment when risk, behaviour or operational context materially changes.

## 28. Implementation Principle

D-AIGAAF governance committees should be adapted to the implementing organisation's:

- legal authority;
- command structure;
- organisational responsibilities;
- mission;
- AI maturity;
- risk profile;
- security requirements.

The framework defines governance functions and principles rather than prescribing a single organisational chart.

---

## 29. Summary

D-AIGAAF governance committees provide structured oversight for consequential AI capabilities.

Their role is to bring together operational, technical, assurance, security, legal, policy, information and acquisition perspectives while preserving clear accountability.

The central rule is:

> **Committees provide governance and challenge; authorised human authorities retain decision rights and accountability.**

Governance must remain proportionate, independent where required, evidence-based, traceable and responsive to changes in AI behaviour, mission, environment and risk.
