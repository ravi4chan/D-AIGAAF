# AI Acquisition

## Summary

AI Acquisition defines how defence organisations procure, license, adapt, integrate and sustain AI-enabled capabilities while preserving control over requirements, risk, security, provenance, assurance and operational authority.

D-AIGAAF treats acquisition as a lifecycle governance activity rather than a commercial transaction.

The central principle is:

**Procurement of an AI capability does not transfer responsibility for its operational risk or authority.**

An acquired AI capability must remain traceable from:

**Mission Need → Requirements → Acquisition → Supplier Evidence → Integration → TEVV → Assurance → Risk Acceptance → Operational Authorisation → Employment → Monitoring → Change → Reauthorisation**

---

## 1. Purpose

The AI Acquisition process establishes controls for acquiring:

- AI models;
- AI-enabled software;
- complete AI-enabled systems;
- AI services;
- data-dependent AI capabilities;
- hardware containing AI functionality;
- third-party AI components;
- externally developed or adapted models.

It seeks to ensure that procurement decisions support:

- mission requirements;
- security;
- safety;
- sovereignty;
- supply-chain assurance;
- transparency;
- configuration control;
- lifecycle sustainment;
- TEVV;
- operational assurance.

---

## 2. Core Principle

An AI capability should not be acquired solely because it performs well against a benchmark or supplier demonstration.

Acquisition should establish whether the capability can be:

- integrated;
- tested;
- evaluated;
- secured;
- monitored;
- controlled;
- sustained;
- updated;
- audited;
- assured;
- operationally authorised.

Supplier claims are inputs to assurance, not substitutes for independent evidence.

---

## 3. Acquisition Scope

Acquisition governance should consider the full capability, including where applicable:

- model;
- software;
- hardware;
- data;
- sensors;
- interfaces;
- communications;
- infrastructure;
- external services;
- dependencies;
- security mechanisms;
- human interfaces;
- autonomy mechanisms;
- support arrangements.

The acquired component may behave differently when integrated into the intended operational system.

---

## 4. Acquisition Planning

Before procurement, the organisation should define:

- mission need;
- intended use case;
- operational environment;
- requirements;
- risk profile;
- autonomy expectations;
- security requirements;
- sovereignty requirements;
- interoperability requirements;
- assurance expectations;
- lifecycle support requirements;
- change-control requirements;
- supplier obligations;
- evidence requirements.

Acquisition planning should involve relevant technical, operational, security, legal, assurance and procurement authorities.

---

## 5. Acquisition Requirements

Procurement specifications should include requirements for:

- functional performance;
- operational performance;
- reliability;
- robustness;
- security;
- data;
- human control;
- autonomy;
- auditability;
- configuration;
- supply-chain provenance;
- updates;
- vulnerabilities;
- incident notification;
- testing;
- evaluation;
- documentation;
- sustainment;
- retirement.

Requirements should be sufficiently specific to permit objective assessment.

---

## 6. Supplier Claims

Suppliers may provide claims regarding:

- performance;
- accuracy;
- reliability;
- security;
- robustness;
- autonomy;
- safety;
- compliance;
- certifications;
- operational suitability.

These claims should be classified as:

**Supplier Claim → Evidence Supplied → Evidence Reviewed → Evidence Independently Verified → Assurance Judgement**

A supplier statement should not automatically become an organisational assurance conclusion.

---

## 7. Supplier Evidence

Supplier evidence should identify, where applicable:

- capability tested;
- model/version;
- configuration;
- data;
- test environment;
- test method;
- test conditions;
- sample size;
- limitations;
- known failure modes;
- evaluation results;
- security assessment;
- dependencies;
- date;
- evidence owner.

Evidence should be evaluated for relevance to the proposed operational environment.

---

## 8. Provenance

Acquisition should establish sufficient provenance for material AI components.

This may include:

- developer;
- model origin;
- training or adaptation origin where available;
- software components;
- hardware;
- datasets;
- third-party libraries;
- external services;
- critical dependencies;
- update mechanisms.

The objective is to identify who can materially affect the behaviour or integrity of the capability.

---

## 9. Supply-Chain Risk

Supply-chain assessment should consider:

- dependency concentration;
- supplier access;
- hidden dependencies;
- third-party components;
- unsupported software;
- vulnerability exposure;
- update mechanisms;
- supplier changes;
- ownership changes;
- service discontinuity;
- jurisdictional considerations;
- provenance gaps.

Critical dependencies should be identified before operational authorisation.

---

## 10. Sovereignty Considerations

Where relevant, acquisition should assess the organisation's ability to retain appropriate control over:

- critical models;
- data;
- software;
- infrastructure;
- configuration;
- updates;
- security;
- operational deployment;
- sustainment.

Sovereignty should be considered as a risk and dependency dimension rather than assumed solely from procurement location.

---

## 11. Intellectual Property and Access

Acquisition arrangements should clarify appropriate access to:

- source code where required;
- model artefacts where required;
- configuration;
- interfaces;
- documentation;
- logs;
- test results;
- security information;
- vulnerability information;
- update information.

The objective is not necessarily to require unrestricted disclosure, but to ensure that the organisation has sufficient information and access to conduct assurance, security, maintenance and incident investigation.

---

## 12. Data Rights and Governance

Contracts should address applicable rights and controls concerning:

- training data;
- operational data;
- user-generated data;
- evaluation data;
- derived data;
- data retention;
- data deletion;
- data location;
- permitted use;
- secondary use;
- data sharing;
- confidentiality.

Data obligations should remain consistent with applicable law, policy and security requirements.

---

## 13. Security Requirements

Acquisition should establish security requirements for:

- model integrity;
- software integrity;
- hardware;
- data;
- interfaces;
- authentication;
- access control;
- logging;
- vulnerability management;
- updates;
- external services;
- supply-chain dependencies.

Security requirements should cover the entire lifecycle, not only initial delivery.

---

## 14. Update and Change Rights

Acquisition arrangements should establish how updates are controlled.

They should address:

- who may issue updates;
- notification requirements;
- update provenance;
- version identification;
- security validation;
- performance assessment;
- testing;
- rollback;
- change classification;
- emergency updates;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

An update should not automatically be considered operationally acceptable merely because it is labelled a patch, minor release or maintenance update.

---

## 15. Behaviourally Significant Changes

Change significance should be based on effect rather than naming convention.

A change may be material if it affects:

- model behaviour;
- decision behaviour;
- autonomy;
- human control;
- safety;
- security;
- information integrity;
- operational performance;
- dependencies;
- assurance evidence.

Changes affecting hidden or opaque model behaviour may require more extensive revalidation even where the supplier describes them as minor.

---

## 16. Vendor Lock-In

Acquisition should assess risks arising from dependence on a single supplier or proprietary ecosystem.

Consider:

- portability;
- data portability;
- model portability;
- interface standards;
- alternative suppliers;
- replacement feasibility;
- documentation;
- escrow arrangements where appropriate;
- long-term support;
- exit costs.

Vendor lock-in can become an operational resilience and sovereignty risk.

---

## 17. Interoperability

Acquisition should specify interoperability requirements for:

- data formats;
- interfaces;
- sensors;
- information systems;
- command systems;
- communications;
- identity systems;
- logging;
- monitoring.

Interoperability should include semantic and operational compatibility, not merely technical connectivity.

---

## 18. Contractual Assurance Obligations

Contracts should establish supplier responsibilities for, as applicable:

- documentation;
- evidence provision;
- security notifications;
- vulnerability disclosure;
- incident notification;
- material change notification;
- dependency notification;
- model updates;
- configuration changes;
- support;
- testing;
- evaluation;
- audit support;
- regulatory or policy changes.

These obligations should continue throughout the agreed support period.

---

## 19. Acceptance Criteria

Commercial delivery acceptance should be separated from operational authorisation.

### Commercial Acceptance

Determines whether the supplier has delivered according to the contractual terms.

### Technical Acceptance

Determines whether technical delivery requirements are satisfied.

### Assurance

Determines whether evidence supports relevant claims and risk judgements.

### Risk Acceptance

Determines whether defined residual risk is acceptable to the appropriately empowered authority.

### Operational Authorisation

Determines whether the capability may be employed in a defined operational context.

These are distinct decisions.

---

## 20. Acquisition Testing

Acquired capabilities should undergo testing appropriate to their consequence and intended use.

Testing may address:

- functional performance;
- reliability;
- robustness;
- security;
- adversarial resilience;
- human interaction;
- autonomy;
- environmental performance;
- interoperability;
- fail-safe behaviour;
- recovery.

Supplier testing may contribute evidence but should not automatically replace independent or acceptance testing where such testing is required.

---

## 21. Acquisition and TEVV

Acquisition should define the evidence required before formal TEVV and assurance.

The evidence chain may be:

**Supplier Evidence → Acceptance Testing → Independent TEVV → Assurance → Risk Acceptance → Operational Authorisation**

The strength of required independent evidence should increase with:

- consequence;
- autonomy;
- uncertainty;
- operational exposure;
- dependency;
- security risk.

---

## 22. Acquisition and Human Authority

Acquired systems should support the required human authority arrangements.

Procurement requirements may include:

- human approval;
- intervention;
- override;
- monitoring;
- uncertainty communication;
- autonomy controls;
- auditability;
- escalation;
- safe-state mechanisms.

A supplier should not define the organisation's command authority through product configuration.

---

## 23. Acquisition and Autonomy

Acquisition should distinguish between:

- technically possible autonomy;
- intended autonomy;
- tested autonomy;
- assured autonomy;
- authorised autonomy.

The capability should not be purchased or accepted on the assumption that all technically available autonomous functions will be operationally permitted.

The core relationship is:

**Technical Capability → Assessed Autonomy → Assured Autonomy → Authorised Autonomy**

---

## 24. Acquisition and Operational Environment

Procurement should identify the environments in which the capability is expected to operate.

Where relevant, requirements should address:

- terrain;
- weather;
- sensor variation;
- data degradation;
- communications degradation;
- infrastructure availability;
- adversarial conditions;
- mission tempo;
- human workload.

Supplier demonstrations performed in controlled environments should not be assumed to represent operational conditions.

---

## 25. Acquisition Risk Assessment

Acquisition risk should consider:

- mission risk;
- technical risk;
- security risk;
- safety risk;
- autonomy risk;
- human-control risk;
- data risk;
- dependency risk;
- supply-chain risk;
- sovereignty risk;
- legal/policy risk;
- sustainment risk;
- obsolescence risk;
- vendor lock-in;
- uncertainty.

Acquisition risk should feed the broader D-AIGAAF risk process.

---

## 26. Sustainment Requirements

Procurement should consider the capability beyond initial delivery.

Sustainment requirements may address:

- maintenance;
- support;
- spare components;
- software updates;
- model updates;
- security patches;
- vulnerability management;
- retraining;
- performance monitoring;
- configuration management;
- technical documentation;
- workforce competence.

A capability that cannot be safely sustained may not be suitable for operational deployment.

---

## 27. Supplier Incident Obligations

Contracts should establish notification arrangements for significant events such as:

- security vulnerabilities;
- breaches;
- model behaviour changes;
- critical defects;
- unexpected behaviour;
- safety issues;
- supply-chain compromise;
- dependency changes;
- withdrawal of support.

Notification should occur within agreed timeframes appropriate to the consequence of the event.

---

## 28. Supplier Change Governance

Suppliers should notify the organisation of changes capable of materially affecting the capability.

The organisation should retain the authority to determine whether a change requires:

- review;
- targeted testing;
- risk reassessment;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation;
- restriction;
- suspension.

The supplier's change classification should inform but not determine the organisation's governance decision.

---

- supplier evidence;
- provenance records;
- configuration;

- security information;
- test results;
- acceptance records;
- licences;

- update procedures;

---

## 30. Acquisition Exit Criteria

Before an acquired capability progresses toward operational assurance, the responsible authority should establish that:

- contractual requirements are satisfied or deviations documented;
- supplier evidence has been reviewed;
- material dependencies are known;
- provenance is sufficiently understood;
- configuration is controlled;
- security requirements are addressed;
- update mechanisms are understood;
- operational requirements are identified;
- TEVV requirements are defined;
- known limitations are documented;
- sustainment arrangements exist.

Commercial delivery alone is not sufficient.

---

## 31. Operational AI Advisor

The Operational AI Advisor (OAIA) may support acquisition by evaluating the relationship between:

- supplier claims;
- technical capability;
- mission need;
- operational context;
- risk;
- autonomy;
- human control;
- assurance evidence.

The OAIA can help identify gaps between what a supplier demonstrates and what the mission actually requires.

The OAIA advises.

**The appropriately authorised authority decides.**

---

## 32. Acquisition Failure Modes

Common failures include:

- buying technology without a defined mission need;
- accepting supplier claims as assurance;
- relying solely on benchmarks;
- inadequate provenance;
- hidden dependencies;
- insufficient access for assurance;
- uncontrolled supplier updates;
- vendor lock-in;
- inadequate interoperability;
- weak incident notification obligations;
- inadequate sustainment;
- treating commercial acceptance as operational authorisation;
- failing to assess actual autonomy;
- testing only supplier-selected conditions;
- failing to preserve configuration traceability;
- accepting proprietary opacity without compensating assurance mechanisms.

## 34. Core Rules

1. **Acquisition must begin from a defined mission need and requirements baseline.**
2. **Supplier claims are not equivalent to assurance.**
3. **The full AI-enabled capability must be assessed.**
4. **Material dependencies and provenance must be identified.**
5. **The organisation must retain sufficient control to test, secure, monitor and govern the capability.**
6. **Commercial acceptance is distinct from operational authorisation.**
7. **Supplier changes must be subject to organisational change governance.**
8. **Operational autonomy must be authorised independently of technical capability.**
9. **Security and supply-chain requirements must extend throughout the lifecycle.**
10. **Sustainment and exit must be considered before acquisition.**
11. **Acquired capabilities must be evaluated in relevant operational environments.**
12. **Configuration must remain traceable from acquisition through operational employment.**
13. **Contracts should establish appropriate obligations for evidence, incidents, vulnerabilities and material changes.**
14. **Acquisition decisions should account for uncertainty and long-term dependency risk.**

---

## 35. Golden Thread

AI Acquisition maintains the Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Acquisition → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Reauthorisation**

Acquisition provides the controlled bridge between requirements and an externally or internally supplied capability that can subsequently be independently tested, assured and authorised.
