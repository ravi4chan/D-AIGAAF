# D-AIGAAF — Sovereignty, Strategic Dependency & Continuity

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes a framework for identifying and managing strategic dependencies that could affect the long-term ability to control, sustain, secure, modify, or replace Defence AI capabilities.

Sovereignty in D-AIGAAF is treated as a risk and resilience consideration rather than as a requirement for complete domestic ownership of every technology or component.

## 2. Sovereignty Objectives

A Defence AI capability should, where proportionate to its mission and risk, retain sufficient ability to:

- Understand critical dependencies
- Exercise operational authority
- Protect critical data
- Verify critical components
- Control material changes
- Sustain essential functions
- Respond to compromise
- Recover from dependency failure
- Replace or isolate critical components where feasible
- Conduct appropriate assurance independently

## 3. Strategic Dependency

A dependency becomes strategically significant when loss, compromise, manipulation, or denial of access could materially affect:

- National or mission-critical capability
- Operational continuity
- Security
- Safety
- Autonomy
- Critical decision support
- Ability to conduct assurance
- Ability to maintain or reauthorise the system

Strategic dependencies should be explicitly identified and risk assessed.

## 4. Dependency Categories

Strategic dependencies may arise from:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Model | External model provider or proprietary model |
| Compute | Specialised processors or constrained compute supply |
| Software | Critical frameworks or proprietary components |
| Data | External datasets or essential data services |
| Infrastructure | Cloud, hosting, or specialised infrastructure |
| Communications | External communication or connectivity dependency |
| Support | Vendor-controlled maintenance or technical expertise |
| Updates | External control over software or model updates |
| Assurance | Inability to independently inspect or test critical components |

## 5. Continuity Assessment

For each strategically important dependency, assess:

1. What happens if the dependency becomes unavailable?
2. How quickly would operational effects emerge?
3. Can the capability operate in a degraded state?
4. Is an alternative available?
5. Can the dependency be replaced?
6. Can the component be isolated?
7. Can the capability operate without external connectivity where required?
8. What recovery resources are available?
9. What authority is required to invoke contingency measures?

## 6. Operational Continuity

Continuity planning should account for degraded and contested environments where applicable.

Depending on the mission, planning may consider:

- Loss of communications
- Loss of external services
- Infrastructure failure
- Supply disruption
- Cyber compromise
- Model or software failure
- Sensor degradation
- Loss of technical support
- Restricted access to updates

The system should have defined degraded modes appropriate to its authorised use.

## 7. Substitutability

Critical dependencies should be assessed for substitutability.

Possible approaches include:

- Alternative supplier
- Alternative model
- Alternative software component
- Alternative hardware
- Local capability
- Offline capability
- Manual fallback
- Redundant architecture
- Pre-positioned recovery resources

Where no practical alternative exists, the dependency should be explicitly recognised as residual risk.

## 8. Strategic Dependency Risk Rating

Risk should consider:

**Dependency Criticality × Consequence × Exposure × Recoverability × Substitutability**

A dependency with high consequence, high exposure, low substitutability, and poor recoverability should receive enhanced governance and assurance.

## 9. Sovereignty and Operational Authority

External suppliers or technical dependencies should not silently acquire operational authority.

The responsible defence organisation should retain authority over:

- Mission definition
- Operational employment
- Autonomy authorisation
- Configuration approval
- Security decisions
- Suspension
- Reauthorisation
- Retirement

Technical dependency must not become delegated operational authority by default.

## 10. Continuity Controls

Possible controls include:

- Supplier diversification
- Component redundancy
- Alternative sourcing
- Secure local repositories
- Verified recovery baselines
- Offline operating capability where required
- Manual fallback procedures
- Controlled spare capacity
- Independent technical expertise
- Lifecycle support arrangements

Controls should be proportionate to risk and operational necessity.

## 11. Strategic Dependency Monitoring

Dependencies should be monitored for:

- Supply disruption
- Supplier ownership changes
- Geopolitical developments
- Export or access restrictions
- Product discontinuation
- Licensing changes
- Vulnerabilities
- Loss of support
- Changes in technical architecture
- Increased concentration

Changes should feed into the D-AIGAAF risk and reauthorisation processes.

## 12. Continuity Exercises

Critical capabilities should periodically test relevant continuity assumptions.

Exercises may evaluate:

- Loss of supplier support
- Loss of connectivity
- Component failure
- Compromised dependency
- Loss of external services
- Recovery from a trusted baseline
- Transition to an alternative capability
- Manual fallback

Exercise findings should be incorporated into assurance and risk records.

## 13. Sovereignty Does Not Mean Isolation

D-AIGAAF should not interpret sovereignty as requiring complete technological isolation.

Interoperability, commercial technology, international supply chains, open-source software, and external expertise may provide substantial operational value.

The objective is instead to ensure that critical dependencies are:

- Known
- Assessed
- Governed
- Secured
- Monitored
- Replaceable or mitigated where feasible
- Reflected in residual risk

## 14. Core Principle

> **Operational sovereignty is the ability to retain meaningful control, assurance, continuity, and decision authority despite dependence on external technologies, organisations, or infrastructure.**

Strategic dependency should therefore be treated as an explicit risk within Defence AI governance rather than as an assumption hidden within procurement or system architecture.
