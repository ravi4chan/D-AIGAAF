# D-AIGAAF — Human Authority Governance Model

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the human authority framework for Defence AI systems where AI-generated information, recommendations, decisions, or actions may affect personnel, operations, property, mission outcomes, or human life.

The framework ensures that responsibility for consequential employment remains identifiable and that AI capability does not silently displace legitimate human authority.

## 2. Core Principle

> **AI may inform, recommend, or act only within authority that has been explicitly defined, assessed, and authorised.**

The presence of an AI system does not by itself transfer command authority from a human decision maker to the system.

## 3. Human Authority Objectives

D-AIGAAF should ensure that:

- Human responsibility remains identifiable.
- Authority for consequential decisions is explicitly assigned.
- AI recommendations can be accepted, rejected, or overridden where applicable.
- Autonomy is separately authorised from capability deployment.
- Human operators understand relevant AI limitations and uncertainty.
- Decisions leading to significant consequences remain traceable.
- AI failure does not create an uncontrolled authority gap.
- Emergency procedures preserve appropriate human accountability.

## 4. Human Authority Chain

The human authority model should distinguish between:

1. **Command Authority** — authority responsible for the mission or operational decision.
2. **Operational AI Advisor (OAIA)** — human bridge between operational command and AI system developers/system owners, providing AI and operational interpretation.
3. **AI System Owner** — accountable for the capability, configuration, lifecycle, and assurance status.
4. **AI Developer / Technical Team** — responsible for development, technical implementation, and technical remediation.
5. **Operator / User** — person interacting with or employing the AI capability within authorised conditions.
6. **Assurance Authority** — evaluates evidence and assurance sufficiency.
7. **Authorising Authority** — grants, restricts, suspends, or revokes operational authorisation.

These roles may be combined or separated depending on organisational structure, but the associated responsibilities should remain identifiable.

## 5. Operational AI Advisor

The OAIA is a key human role within D-AIGAAF.

The role exists because AI developers may not understand every operational context, while commanders may not possess sufficient knowledge of AI limitations, uncertainty, failure modes, or system behaviour.

The OAIA should help bridge this gap by:

- Interpreting AI capability and limitations in operational context.
- Advising commanders on appropriate use.
- Identifying material uncertainty or anomalous behaviour.
- Supporting assessment of AI recommendations.
- Advising on autonomy boundaries.
- Facilitating communication with system owners and technical teams.
- Supporting incident, revalidation, and reauthorisation processes.

The OAIA advises command; the role does not replace command authority.

## 6. Decision Rights

For consequential use cases, D-AIGAAF should identify:

- Who may request AI support.
- Who may rely on an AI recommendation.
- Who may approve an AI-supported action.
- Who may authorise autonomous behaviour.
- Who may override or suspend the system.
- Who may change operational constraints.
- Who may accept residual risk.
- Who may revoke operational use.

Decision rights should be established before operational employment.

## 7. AI as Advisor by Default

Unless a specific autonomy level has been separately authorised:

> **AI should remain advisory or decision-support in nature.**

An AI system should not acquire consequential decision authority merely because it is technically capable of performing an action.

Any transition from advisory use toward autonomous action should be subject to the applicable risk, autonomy, assurance, and operational-authorisation processes.

## 8. Accountability and Traceability

For consequential AI-supported decisions, records should make it possible to determine:

- What the AI system provided.
- What information was available to the human decision maker.
- What uncertainty or limitations were communicated.
- Who reviewed the information.
- Who made the consequential decision.
- Whether the AI recommendation was accepted, modified, or rejected.
- What authority permitted the action.
- What system and configuration baseline was involved.

The objective is not to attribute every outcome to an individual automatically, but to preserve an auditable chain of authority and decision-making.

## 9. Human Override and Bypass

Where human intervention is authorised, personnel should have a meaningful ability to:

- Reject an AI recommendation.
- Override an AI-generated action.
- Suspend AI-supported activity.
- Transition to a safer operating mode.
- Revert to manual procedures.

Where a commander or authorised decision maker rejects an AI recommendation, the system should support appropriate recording of the rationale where operationally and legally required.

## 10. Human Authority Under Degraded Conditions

Human authority arrangements should account for:

- Loss of communications.
- Limited connectivity.
- Sensor degradation.
- Electronic interference.
- Cyber incidents.
- AI malfunction.
- Loss of supporting personnel.
- Delayed access to higher authority.

Predefined authority boundaries should prevent ambiguity when normal command or technical support channels are unavailable.

## 11. Emergency Authority

Where delay could create unacceptable harm, pre-authorised emergency procedures may permit immediate protective action, including suspension, isolation, or transition to a safer mode.

Emergency authority should define:

- Trigger conditions.
- Who may act.
- Permitted actions.
- Required safeguards.
- Subsequent reporting.
- Technical and command review.
- Revalidation or reauthorisation requirements.

Emergency authority should remain bounded and auditable.

## 12. Competence

Personnel exercising authority over Defence AI should possess competence appropriate to their role, including where relevant:

- Mission and operational understanding.
- AI capability and limitation awareness.
- Understanding of uncertainty.
- Risk and autonomy concepts.
- Security awareness.
- Fail-safe and degradation procedures.
- Relevant legal, policy, and organisational requirements.

Higher-consequence or higher-autonomy roles should require proportionately greater competence.

## 13. Authority and AI Uncertainty

AI systems should communicate meaningful uncertainty and relevant limitations in a form that human decision makers can understand.

Human authority should not be considered meaningful if the system:

- Presents uncertain outputs as certain.
- Conceals material limitations.
- Prevents appropriate review.
- Produces recommendations without sufficient context.
- Makes it impractical to understand when its output should not be trusted.

## 14. Governance Principle

Human authority should be treated as an explicit control within Defence AI governance rather than as an assumption.

The framework should establish a continuous relationship:

**AI Capability → Information/Recommendation → Human Interpretation → Authority → Decision/Action → Record → Review**

This preserves human agency while recognising that meaningful human control depends on competence, information, time, authority, and the ability to intervene.
