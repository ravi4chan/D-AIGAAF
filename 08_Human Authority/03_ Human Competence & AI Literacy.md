# D-AIGAAF — Human Competence & AI Literacy

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes competence and AI literacy requirements for personnel who develop, assure, authorise, operate, supervise, or govern Defence AI capabilities.

The objective is to ensure that human authority over AI is supported by sufficient knowledge and practical competence.

## 2. Core Principle

> **The level of human competence required should be proportionate to the consequence, criticality, autonomy, and complexity of the AI capability being governed or employed.**

Human authority without adequate competence is not sufficient to establish meaningful control.

## 3. Competence Domains

Depending on role, personnel should understand relevant aspects of:

- Mission and operational context.
- AI capabilities and limitations.
- Data and information quality.
- AI uncertainty and confidence.
- Common AI failure modes.
- Risk and autonomy.
- Human-AI interaction.
- Cybersecurity and AI security.
- Privacy and information protection.
- Legal, policy, and ethical requirements.
- TEVV and assurance.
- Incident and fail-safe procedures.
- Change and reauthorisation processes.

No single individual is expected to master every domain. Competence should be distributed across the governance structure while critical decisions retain access to the required expertise.

## 4. Role-Based Competence

Competence requirements should be defined by role.

| Role | Indicative competence |
|---|---|
| Command Authority | Mission risk, AI limitations, uncertainty, authority and accountability |
| Operational AI Advisor | Operational knowledge plus strong AI, risk, assurance and human-control knowledge |
| Operator / User | System operation, limitations, uncertainty, intervention and safety procedures |
| System Owner | Lifecycle, technical risk, security, assurance and configuration management |
| Developer | AI engineering, security, testing, operational requirements and applicable governance |
| Assurance Personnel | TEVV, risk, evidence evaluation and operational context |
| Authorising Authority | Risk, assurance, legal/policy requirements and operational authority |

Organisations should tailor requirements to actual responsibilities.

## 5. AI Literacy

AI literacy should enable personnel to understand that AI systems:

- Do not inherently understand the mission.
- Can produce incorrect outputs.
- May behave differently under changed conditions.
- Depend on data and system configuration.
- Can express unwarranted confidence.
- May fail in ways not anticipated during development.
- Require defined operating boundaries.
- Should not automatically be treated as authoritative.

AI literacy should focus on practical decision-making rather than requiring every user to become an AI engineer.

## 6. Operational AI Advisor Competence

The Operational AI Advisor should possess a combined understanding of:

- Operational planning and decision-making.
- AI system capabilities.
- AI limitations and failure modes.
- Data and model behaviour.
- Risk and autonomy.
- Human-AI interaction.
- Security concerns.
- TEVV and assurance evidence.
- Operational authorisation.

The OAIA should be capable of translating between technical teams and operational commanders.

## 7. Competence for Autonomous Systems

Personnel supervising higher-autonomy functions should additionally understand:

- Authorised autonomy level.
- Operating boundaries.
- Transition conditions.
- Human intervention mechanisms.
- Failure and degradation behaviour.
- Conditions that invalidate authorisation.
- Appropriate response to unexpected behaviour.

The required competence should increase as autonomy and consequence increase.

## 8. Training

Training should combine:

- Classroom or digital learning.
- Practical system training.
- Scenario-based exercises.
- Failure-mode exercises.
- Human-AI decision exercises.
- Override and intervention drills.
- Incident response exercises.
- Periodic refresher training.

Training should reflect the actual operational context in which the AI will be used.

## 9. Scenario-Based Competence

Competence should be tested under conditions that may include:

- Correct AI outputs.
- Incorrect AI outputs.
- Ambiguous information.
- High uncertainty.
- Conflicting data.
- System degradation.
- Communications loss.
- Cyber or security events.
- Unexpected autonomous behaviour.
- Time pressure.
- Requirement to reject or override AI.

The objective is to test judgement, not simply knowledge recall.

## 10. Competence and Authorisation

Personnel exercising defined AI decision rights should meet applicable competence requirements before receiving those rights.

For higher-consequence roles, organisations should consider:

- Formal qualification.
- Practical assessment.
- Periodic recertification.
- Role-specific training.
- Experience requirements.
- Continuing professional development.

## 11. Competence Records

Relevant records should include:

- Training completed.
- Qualification or certification.
- Practical assessment.
- Role and authority.
- Date of assessment.
- Expiry or review date where applicable.
- Remedial training.
- Significant changes to required competence.

Competence records should support auditability without unnecessarily exposing personal information.

## 12. Maintaining Competence

Competence should be reviewed when:

- The AI capability materially changes.
- Autonomy increases.
- Mission context changes.
- New failure modes emerge.
- Major incidents occur.
- Operating conditions materially change.
- New legal or policy requirements apply.

A material change in capability may therefore require both system revalidation and personnel retraining.

## 13. Competence Under Degraded Conditions

Training should account for environments where:

- Communications are intermittent.
- Technical support is unavailable.
- Sensor inputs are degraded.
- AI outputs become uncertain.
- Manual fallback is required.
- Personnel must operate under time pressure.

Personnel should understand what the system can and cannot safely do under degraded conditions.

## 14. Organisational Competence

Human competence should not depend entirely on one individual.

Organisations should maintain sufficient collective expertise to support:

- Operational decisions.
- Technical troubleshooting.
- Assurance.
- Security response.
- Incident investigation.
- Revalidation.
- Reauthorisation.

Critical roles should have suitable succession and continuity arrangements.

## 15. Core Principle

D-AIGAAF treats AI literacy and human competence as governance controls, not merely training requirements.

The desired outcome is:

**Competent Human → Calibrated Understanding → Appropriate Trust → Informed Decision → Effective Control → Accountable Action**
