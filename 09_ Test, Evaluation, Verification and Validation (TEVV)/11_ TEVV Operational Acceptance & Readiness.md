# D-AIGAAF — TEVV Operational Acceptance & Readiness

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how TEVV results are consolidated into an assessment of whether a Defence AI capability is ready to proceed to operational authorisation and employment.

The objective is to ensure that readiness is determined from evidence across technical, human, security, autonomy, environmental and mission dimensions rather than from a single test result.

## 2. Core Principle

> **Operational readiness is an evidence-based judgement that the capability is sufficiently understood, assured and controlled for its proposed employment.**

Readiness does not mean that the system is risk-free or universally suitable.

## 3. Readiness Assessment

The readiness assessment should consider:

- Approved mission and use case.
- Operational environment.
- Risk profile.
- Autonomy level.
- Human authority.
- Technical performance.
- Reliability and robustness.
- Security.
- Human-AI interaction.
- Mission effectiveness.
- Evidence completeness.
- Known limitations.
- Residual risk.
- Required controls.

## 4. Readiness Conditions

Before operational authorisation, the capability should demonstrate that:

1. The intended mission is clearly defined.
2. Requirements are established and traceable.
3. Relevant risks have been assessed.
4. Appropriate controls are implemented.
5. TEVV has been conducted at a level proportionate to risk.
6. Significant findings are resolved or formally dispositioned.
7. Human roles and decision rights are established.
8. Operational limitations are understood.
9. Fail-safe and intervention mechanisms are demonstrated.
10. Required evidence is available for authorisation.

## 5. TEVV Dimension Review

Readiness should consider the eight D-AIGAAF TEVV dimensions:

1. Technical Performance.
2. Reliability & Robustness.
3. Adversarial Resilience.
4. Operational Environment.
5. Human-AI Interaction.
6. Security & Integrity.
7. Autonomy & Control.
8. Mission Effectiveness.

Each dimension should have an appropriate evidence basis.

## 6. Readiness Categories

D-AIGAAF may use the following working categories:

- **Not Ready** — evidence or controls are insufficient.
- **Conditionally Ready** — readiness exists only under defined restrictions.
- **Ready for Authorisation** — evidence supports consideration by the authorising authority.
- **Ready for Restricted Employment** — employment is supported within a limited operational envelope.
- **Ready for Approved Employment** — evidence supports the proposed employment within the authorised envelope.

These are framework constructs and should be aligned with applicable organisational terminology.

## 7. Open Findings

Open findings should be classified according to whether they:

- Prevent readiness.
- Permit only restricted employment.
- Require additional monitoring.
- Require compensating controls.
- Can be accepted by the appropriate authority.

High-consequence unresolved findings should receive explicit authorising-authority attention.

## 8. Evidence Gaps

An evidence gap should prevent an unsupported assumption from being treated as an assurance conclusion.

For each material gap, the readiness assessment should state:

- What is unknown.
- Why it matters.
- Associated risk.
- Existing mitigation.
- Additional evidence required.
- Responsible owner.
- Target resolution.

## 9. Operational Limitations

Readiness documentation should explicitly state limitations such as:

- Environmental boundaries.
- Sensor limitations.
- Data limitations.
- Communications requirements.
- Human supervision requirements.
- Autonomy restrictions.
- Known failure modes.
- Performance boundaries.

Limitations should be capable of being translated into operational controls where necessary.

## 10. Human Readiness

Operational readiness should include confirmation that required personnel:

- Understand their roles.
- Have appropriate competence.
- Understand AI limitations.
- Can interpret uncertainty.
- Can intervene.
- Can override where authorised.
- Know fail-safe procedures.
- Know escalation and incident procedures.

A system should not be considered operationally ready if the required human control cannot be reliably exercised.

## 11. Security Readiness

Security readiness should consider whether:

- Critical security controls are implemented.
- Security findings are appropriately treated.
- Model and configuration integrity are protected.
- Critical dependencies are understood.
- Incident response arrangements exist.
- Security monitoring is available where required.

Security readiness should reflect the current system baseline and threat context.

## 12. Autonomy Readiness

Where autonomy is proposed, readiness should establish that:

- The authorised autonomy level is explicit.
- Actual behaviour has been evaluated.
- Boundaries are enforced.
- Human intervention is effective where required.
- Transitions are understood.
- Fail-safe behaviour is demonstrated.
- Degraded conditions have been considered.

Higher autonomy should require stronger evidence and control.

## 13. Configuration Readiness

The capability presented for authorisation should correspond to a controlled configuration baseline.

The readiness record should identify, where relevant:

- Model version.
- Software version.
- Hardware configuration.
- Data dependencies.
- Critical interfaces.
- Security controls.
- Supporting services.

Material differences between the tested configuration and proposed operational configuration should be assessed.

## 14. Operational Trial Completion

Where operational trials are required, readiness should confirm:

- Trial objectives were met.
- Acceptance criteria were assessed.
- Significant deviations were recorded.
- Limitations were identified.
- Evidence was preserved.
- Outstanding issues were dispositioned.

Completion of a trial should not automatically imply readiness.

## 15. Independent Review

The readiness assessment should incorporate the results of appropriate independent evaluation and review.

For higher-consequence capabilities, independent scrutiny should be proportionate to:

- Consequence.
- Autonomy.
- Complexity.
- Novelty.
- Uncertainty.

## 16. Readiness Statement

The final readiness statement should clearly answer:

- What capability is being assessed?
- For which mission?
- Under which conditions?
- At what autonomy level?
- Based on what evidence?
- With what limitations?
- With what residual risk?
- What controls are required?
- Is it ready for consideration of operational authorisation?

The statement should not claim broader readiness than the evidence supports.

## 17. Handover to Operational Authorisation

Once TEVV readiness is established, the relevant evidence package should be transferred into the operational authorisation process.

The relationship is:

**TEVV → Readiness Assessment → Assurance Package → Operational Authorisation**

Operational authorisation remains a separate authority decision.

## 18. Readiness Review

Before authorisation, a formal readiness review should confirm that:

- Evidence remains current.
- System baseline is unchanged or assessed.
- Risks remain acceptable.
- Controls remain effective.
- Conditions are understood.
- Required personnel are prepared.
- Outstanding findings are appropriately dispositioned.

## 19. Revalidation

Readiness should not be treated as permanent.

Revalidation should be triggered by material changes to:

- Model.
- Data.
- Software.
- Hardware.
- Mission.
- Environment.
- Autonomy.
- Human roles.
- Security posture.
- Critical dependencies.

## 20. Core Rule

> **Operational readiness is the point at which the evidence package is sufficiently complete and credible to support an informed authorisation decision—not the point at which all uncertainty has disappeared.**

The readiness assessment therefore acts as the final TEVV bridge between evidence and operational authority.
