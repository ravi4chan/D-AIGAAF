# D-AIGAAF — Operational Authorisation Governance Model

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the governance model for operationally authorising Defence AI capabilities.

Operational authorisation establishes whether a specific AI capability may be employed for a defined mission, within defined environmental conditions, at a defined autonomy level and under defined human authority.

## 2. Core Principle

> **Operational authorisation is the formal decision that an AI capability is sufficiently assured and controlled for a specific authorised use—not a declaration that the AI system is universally safe or reliable.**

Authorisation should therefore be contextual, evidence-based and attributable to an appropriate authority.

## 3. Authorisation Object

The fundamental authorisation relationship is:

**AI Capability × Mission × Environment × Autonomy × Human Authority**

Changing any of these dimensions may change the basis for authorisation.

## 4. Purpose of Authorisation

Operational authorisation should establish:

- What the capability may do.
- For which mission or use case.
- Under which conditions.
- At what autonomy level.
- Under whose authority.
- Within what operational boundaries.
- Based on what evidence.
- With what restrictions and safeguards.
- For how long.
- Under what conditions authorisation may be suspended or revoked.

## 5. Authorisation Principles

Operational authorisation should be:

### Contextual

Authorised for defined missions and environments.

### Evidence-based

Supported by appropriate TEVV and assurance evidence.

### Risk-proportionate

More consequential or autonomous functions require stronger assurance and authority.

### Traceable

The decision and its supporting evidence should be recorded.

### Conditional where necessary

Restrictions and operating conditions should be explicit.

### Reversible

Authorisation should permit suspension, restriction, modification or revocation.

### Human-accountable

The designated authority remains responsible for the authorisation decision.

## 6. Governance Layers

Operational authorisation should connect:

1. Strategy and policy.
2. Mission and use case.
3. Risk and autonomy.
4. AI lifecycle.
5. Data and information.
6. AI security.
7. Supply chain and sovereignty.
8. Human authority.
9. TEVV.
10. Operational environment.
11. Operational employment.
12. Continuous assurance.

## 7. Authorisation Inputs

The authorising authority should receive sufficient information to make an informed decision.

Inputs may include:

- Mission definition.
- Use case.
- Operational context.
- Risk assessment.
- Autonomy assessment.
- Human-control assessment.
- AI lifecycle records.
- Data assurance.
- Security assurance.
- Supply-chain assessment.
- TEVV evidence.
- Operational environment assessment.
- Environmental readiness.
- Known limitations.
- Fail-safe and fallback arrangements.
- Monitoring arrangements.
- Incident and lessons-learned information.

## 8. Assurance Before Authorisation

Authorisation should not substitute for assurance.

The authorisation decision should rely on an assurance record that establishes:

- What was tested.
- Under what conditions.
- What the evidence demonstrates.
- What remains uncertain.
- What limitations remain.
- What risks remain.
- What controls are in place.

## 9. Authorisation States

A common governance state model is:

**Proposed → Under Assurance → Conditionally Authorised → Operationally Authorised → Restricted → Suspended → Revoked/Retired**

Organisations may adapt these states to their own governance structures.

## 10. Conditional Authorisation

Conditional authorisation may be appropriate where the capability can be safely employed subject to explicit restrictions.

Conditions may include:

- Restricted environment.
- Restricted mission.
- Reduced autonomy.
- Increased human supervision.
- Additional monitoring.
- Limited duration.
- Specific configuration.
- Additional confirmation requirements.

Conditions should be measurable and enforceable where practicable.

## 11. Operational Authorisation

Operational authorisation should specify the approved combination of:

- Capability.
- Mission.
- Environment.
- Autonomy.
- Human authority.
- Operating envelope.
- Dependencies.
- Safeguards.
- Monitoring.
- Restrictions.

The authorisation should not exceed the evidence base.

## 12. Restricted Status

A capability may be placed in restricted status when:

- Risk increases.
- Environmental conditions change.
- Assurance evidence becomes uncertain.
- New incidents arise.
- Dependencies degrade.
- Human-control conditions deteriorate.

Restriction may be temporary or remain until specific conditions are restored.

## 13. Suspension

Suspension should be available when continued operation cannot be justified.

Potential triggers include:

- Serious incident.
- Material assurance failure.
- Loss of meaningful human control.
- Critical security concern.
- Operation outside authorised boundaries.
- Unacceptable performance degradation.
- Loss of critical safeguards.
- Material change without required reassessment.

## 14. Revocation and Retirement

Revocation removes operational authorisation where the capability is no longer justified for the authorised use.

Retirement is the lifecycle decision to cease use of the capability and transition toward retirement and decommissioning.

Revocation may occur without immediate technical retirement.

## 15. Authorising Authority

The appropriate authorising authority should be determined by:

- Consequence.
- Mission criticality.
- Autonomy.
- Risk.
- Operational scope.
- Applicable law and policy.

The authority should have sufficient operational and governance competence to understand the decision being made.

## 16. Separation of Responsibilities

Where appropriate, the framework should distinguish between:

- Developer.
- System owner.
- Technical authority.
- Assurance authority.
- Operational AI Advisor.
- Command authority.
- Authorising authority.

No single role should be assumed to possess all responsibilities merely because it controls the technology.

## 17. Operational AI Advisor

The Operational AI Advisor may support authorisation by translating between:

**Operational mission needs ↔ AI capability ↔ Risk ↔ Evidence ↔ Operational implications**

The advisor may identify uncertainty, limitations, evidence gaps and implications for autonomy or human control.

The advisor does not replace command or formal authorisation authority unless separately designated under applicable governance arrangements.

## 18. Authorisation Record

The operational authorisation record should contain, as appropriate:

- Authorisation identifier.
- Capability.
- Mission/use case.
- Environment.
- Autonomy level.
- Human authority.
- Operational boundaries.
- Evidence basis.
- Known limitations.
- Residual risks.
- Safeguards.
- Monitoring requirements.
- Fail-safe arrangements.
- Dependencies.
- Restrictions.
- Authorising authority.
- Effective period.
- Review date.
- Suspension/revocation triggers.

## 19. Validity and Review

Authorisation should have an appropriate review mechanism.

Review may be triggered by:

- Scheduled review.
- Material system change.
- Environmental change.
- Mission change.
- Significant incident.
- New threat.
- New evidence.
- Change in autonomy.
- Change in human-control arrangements.
- Material dependency change.

## 20. Emergency Protective Action

The framework should allow pre-authorised emergency procedures where immediate protective action is necessary to prevent unacceptable harm.

Emergency action should be:

- Clearly bounded.
- Appropriate to the situation.
- Recorded.
- Subject to subsequent review.

Emergency action should not create unrestricted authority for AI to act beyond its authorised role.

## 21. Authorisation and AI Behaviour

The existence of an authorisation should not be interpreted as proof that the AI system will always behave as expected.

Operational authorisation should therefore include:

- Known failure modes.
- Uncertainty.
- Environmental limitations.
- Human intervention requirements.
- Fail-safe behaviour.
- Monitoring requirements.

## 22. Change and Reauthorisation

Changes that may materially affect:

- Model behaviour.
- Hidden model layers.
- Decision logic.
- Autonomy.
- Environmental assumptions.
- Human control.
- Security.
- Critical dependencies.

should undergo the applicable change-impact assessment and, where required, revalidation, additional trials and reauthorisation.

Minor administrative or non-material changes may follow a proportionate process.

## 23. Continuous Assurance

Operational authorisation is not the end of assurance.

During authorised employment, new information should feed into:

**Monitoring → Risk → Assurance → Authorisation → Operational Control**

Material changes should trigger the appropriate governance response.

## 24. Governance Questions

The authorising authority should be able to answer:

1. What exactly is being authorised?
2. For which mission?
3. In which environment?
4. At what autonomy level?
5. Under whose human authority?
6. What evidence supports the decision?
7. What remains uncertain?
8. What restrictions apply?
9. What happens if conditions change?
10. Who can suspend or revoke authorisation?

## 25. Core Rule

> **Operational authorisation binds capability, mission, environment, autonomy, human authority, evidence and risk into one accountable decision.**

An AI capability should not be treated as operationally authorised merely because it has been developed, acquired, tested or technically demonstrated.
