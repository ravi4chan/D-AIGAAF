# 10 Authorisation Exceptions and Derogations

## 1. Purpose

This document defines governance for exceptions and derogations from established D-AIGAAF operational authorisation requirements, conditions, controls or procedures.

Exceptions may be necessary in unusual circumstances, but they shall not become an informal mechanism for bypassing assurance, accountability or operational authority.

---

## 2. Core Principle

**An exception changes the way an established requirement is applied; it does not remove the need for accountable authority, risk assessment, evidence and traceability.**

Exceptions should be:

- Explicit.
- Justified.
- Time-bounded where practicable.
- Risk-assessed.
- Authorised by an appropriate authority.
- Recorded.
- Reviewed.

---

## 3. Definition of Exception

An exception is a formally approved departure from a normally applicable:

- Requirement.
- Control.
- Condition.
- Procedure.
- Evidence expectation.
- Operational restriction.

An exception should identify precisely what requirement is being departed from.

---

## 4. Definition of Derogation

A derogation is a formally approved allowance to operate under an alternative or temporarily reduced requirement where full compliance is impracticable or inappropriate for a defined circumstance.

The distinction between exception and derogation may vary across organisations. D-AIGAAF uses the terms as working governance concepts.

---

## 5. When Exceptions May Be Considered

Exceptions may be considered when:

- Operational circumstances are materially different from normal assumptions.
- Immediate protective action is required.
- A temporary capability limitation exists.
- A required dependency is unavailable.
- A control cannot be implemented as originally designed.
- An alternative control provides equivalent or sufficient risk treatment.
- A temporary transition is required during system change.
- A formal requirement is technically or operationally impracticable in a defined context.

Convenience, schedule pressure or resource constraints alone should not automatically justify an exception for a high-consequence capability.

---

## 6. Prohibited Use of Exceptions

Exceptions shall not be used to:

- Conceal inadequate assurance.
- Avoid required testing.
- Bypass authorisation.
- Circumvent human authority.
- Authorise prohibited activity.
- Conceal a material system change.
- Convert an unauthorised capability into an authorised one without assessment.
- Avoid legal or mandatory requirements where no lawful derogation exists.
- Permanently normalise a temporary weakness.

An exception should not be used to create authority that the approving authority does not otherwise possess.

---

## 7. Exception Request

An exception request should identify:

- Requirement being excepted.
- Capability.
- Mission.
- Environment.
- Configuration.
- Autonomy.
- Human authority.
- Reason for exception.
- Operational necessity.
- Duration.
- Scope.
- Risk.
- Existing controls.
- Compensating controls.
- Evidence.
- Residual risk.
- Responsible owner.
- Proposed review date.
- Proposed exit criteria.

---

## 8. Exception Assessment

The assessment should determine:

1. What requirement is being departed from?
2. Why is the departure necessary?
3. What risk does the departure introduce?
4. What risk was the original requirement intended to control?
5. Are alternative controls available?
6. Is the alternative control sufficiently effective?
7. Does the exception affect human authority?
8. Does it affect autonomy?
9. Does it affect mission or environmental scope?
10. Does it affect security or supply-chain assurance?
11. Does it invalidate existing TEVV evidence?
12. Does it require revalidation or reauthorisation?

---

## 9. Compensating Controls

Where a control cannot be satisfied, alternative controls should be considered.

Examples may include:

- Reduced autonomy.
- Increased human supervision.
- Narrower mission scope.
- Restricted environment.
- Additional monitoring.
- Shorter validity period.
- Additional testing.
- Additional technical controls.
- Reduced system functionality.
- Increased reporting or review frequency.

Compensating controls should address the underlying risk rather than simply document the absence of the original control.

---

## 10. Risk Assessment

An exception should be evaluated against:

- Consequence.
- Mission criticality.
- Autonomy.
- Human control.
- Environmental conditions.
- Security.
- Data integrity.
- Dependency risk.
- Operational uncertainty.
- Reversibility.
- Duration.

Higher-consequence exceptions require stronger justification, evidence and authority.

---

## 11. Exception Authority

The authority approving an exception should be appropriate to its consequence and scope.

The framework should define:

- Who may approve low-risk exceptions.
- Who may approve material exceptions.
- Who may approve emergency exceptions.
- Which exceptions require escalation.
- Which requirements cannot be derogated.

The capability owner should not automatically possess authority to approve exceptions affecting the operational basis of the capability.

---

## 12. Non-Derogable Requirements

Some requirements may be designated non-derogable.

Examples may include requirements relating to:

- Applicable law.
- Fundamental human authority.
- Mandatory safety controls.
- Explicit prohibitions.
- Certain security requirements.
- Core accountability requirements.
- Required operational authorisation.

D-AIGAAF should not imply that every requirement can be waived.

---

## 13. Emergency Exceptions

Emergency conditions may require rapid approval of temporary deviations.

Where immediate action is required:

- Pre-authorised emergency procedures should be used where available.
- The action should remain within defined emergency authority.
- The exception should be recorded.
- The decision should be reviewed retrospectively.
- Continued use should require formal authority.

Emergency exception procedures should not become permanent workarounds.

---

## 14. Temporary Nature

Exceptions should normally have:

- Start date.
- Expiry date or termination condition.
- Defined scope.
- Responsible owner.
- Review date.
- Exit criteria.

An exception that remains necessary over an extended period should be reconsidered as a potential requirement, design or governance change.

---

## 15. Exception Conditions

An approved exception may include conditions such as:

- Reduced autonomy.
- Increased supervision.
- Specific environment.
- Restricted mission.
- Specific configuration.
- Additional monitoring.
- Additional reporting.
- Additional testing.
- Shortened validity.
- Mandatory review after a defined event.

Failure to satisfy an exception condition should trigger the applicable response.

---

## 16. Exception Register

Organisations should maintain an Exception Register containing, as applicable:

- Exception identifier.
- Requirement affected.
- Capability.
- Mission.
- Environment.
- Configuration.
- Autonomy.
- Human authority.
- Reason.
- Risk assessment.
- Compensating controls.
- Approving authority.
- Start date.
- Expiry/review date.
- Conditions.
- Status.
- Evidence.
- Related incidents.
- Closure decision.

---

## 17. Exception Status

A practical status model may include:

- Proposed.
- Under Assessment.
- Approved.
- Approved with Conditions.
- Active.
- Expiring.
- Extended.
- Closed.
- Rejected.
- Withdrawn.

These are D-AIGAAF working categories.

---

## 18. Extension of an Exception

An exception should not be extended automatically.

Extension should require reassessment of:

- Continuing necessity.
- Risk.
- Compensating controls.
- Operational experience.
- New evidence.
- System changes.
- Environmental changes.
- Alternative solutions.

Repeated extensions should trigger governance review.

---

## 19. Exception Closure

An exception should be closed when:

- The original requirement is restored.
- A permanent control replaces the exception.
- The capability is restricted.
- The capability is revalidated.
- The capability is reauthorised under a revised basis.
- The capability is suspended.
- The capability is retired.

Closure should be documented.

---

## 20. Exceptions Affecting Assurance

An exception that affects the assurance basis should trigger an assessment of whether:

- Existing TEVV evidence remains valid.
- Additional testing is required.
- Risk assessment must be updated.
- Operational environment assessment must be repeated.
- Human-control assessment must be repeated.
- Revalidation is required.
- Reauthorisation is required.

An exception should never be assumed to leave assurance evidence unchanged.

---

## 21. Exceptions Affecting Autonomy

Any exception that increases or materially changes autonomy should receive heightened scrutiny.

The assessment should consider:

- New decision authority.
- New failure modes.
- Human supervision.
- Intervention capability.
- Consequence.
- Boundary behaviour.
- Fail-safe behaviour.
- TEVV requirements.

An exception should not be used to grant a higher autonomy level without the appropriate authority and assurance.

---

## 22. Exceptions Affecting Human Authority

An exception must not obscure who remains responsible for consequential decisions.

Where an exception changes:

- Decision rights.
- Supervision.
- Intervention.
- Override.
- Escalation.
- Accountability.

The Authorisation Record should be updated as required.

---

## 23. Exceptions Affecting Configuration

Where an exception permits use of a configuration outside the established baseline:

- The configuration should be uniquely identified.
- The change should be controlled.
- Relevant testing should be assessed.
- Assurance implications should be documented.
- Revalidation or reauthorisation should be initiated where required.

No configuration should become operationally accepted merely through repeated exception use.

---

## 24. Exceptions and Scope

An exception should not silently expand the scope of authority.

If an exception effectively creates:

- A new mission.
- A new use case.
- A new environment.
- A new autonomy level.
- A new user population.
- A new operational area.

the organisation should determine whether formal reauthorisation is required.

---

## 25. Exception Escalation

Escalation should occur where:

- Risk increases.
- Conditions cannot be satisfied.
- The exception is repeatedly extended.
- New incidents occur.
- The exception affects multiple capabilities.
- The exception affects human authority.
- The exception affects legal or policy compliance.
- The exception changes the assurance basis.
- The original justification is no longer valid.

---

## 26. Independence

For high-consequence exceptions, assessment should include appropriate independent input.

Depending on the issue, this may include:

- Technical authority.
- Assurance/TEVV authority.
- Security authority.
- Operational AI Advisor.
- Risk owner.
- Legal or policy authority.
- Operational command authority.

The requesting party should not be the sole source of assurance for its own exception.

---

## 27. Monitoring Active Exceptions

Active exceptions should be monitored for:

- Trigger conditions.
- Risk indicators.
- Control effectiveness.
- Incidents.
- Near misses.
- Performance changes.
- Environmental changes.
- Security events.
- Evidence changes.

Monitoring should determine whether the exception remains justified.

---

## 28. Relationship with Authorisation

An exception does not automatically modify an Operational Authorisation Record.

Where an exception changes the operational permission, the authorisation record should be formally amended or replaced as required.

The relationship between:

**Exception → Risk → Control → Evidence → Assurance → Authority**

should remain traceable.

---

## 29. Golden Thread

Exception governance preserves the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Reauthorisation**

An exception should be traceable to the requirement it affects and to the risk, evidence and authority supporting the decision.

---

## 30. Core Rule

**Exceptions may provide controlled flexibility in unusual circumstances, but no exception should be allowed to obscure risk, dilute human accountability, bypass required assurance or create operational authority beyond what the approving authority is entitled to grant.**
