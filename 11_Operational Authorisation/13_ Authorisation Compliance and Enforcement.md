# 13 Authorisation Compliance and Enforcement

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the governance approach for ensuring that Defence AI capabilities remain within the conditions, restrictions and decision rights established by operational authorisation.

The objective is to identify, prevent, detect, report and address non-compliance before it creates unacceptable operational, safety, security or legal risk.

---

## 2. Core Principle

**Operational authorisation is effective only when its conditions and restrictions are followed. Non-compliance shall be identifiable, accountable, proportionately addressed and traceable to the underlying authorisation.**

---

## 3. Compliance

For D-AIGAAF, compliance means that an AI capability is employed:

- For an authorised mission or use case.
- Within the authorised operational environment.
- At the authorised autonomy level.
- Under the authorised human authority.
- Using the authorised configuration.
- Within defined conditions and restrictions.
- With required monitoring and safeguards.
- Within the authorised validity period.
- In accordance with applicable legal and policy requirements.

---

## 4. Compliance Obligations

The Capability Owner and relevant operational authorities should ensure that:

- Current authorisation status is known.
- Applicable conditions are communicated.
- Personnel understand operational boundaries.
- Configuration remains controlled.
- Required assurance remains current.
- Monitoring is performed.
- Material deviations are reported.
- Records are maintained.
- Corrective action is taken where required.

---

## 5. Authorisation as a Control Boundary

The Operational Authorisation Record establishes a formal control boundary.

Examples of potential boundary violations include:

- Use outside an authorised mission.
- Use outside the approved environment.
- Use at a higher autonomy level.
- Use with an unauthorised configuration.
- Use after authorisation expiry.
- Use despite a suspension.
- Failure to maintain required human supervision.
- Failure to comply with mandatory safeguards.
- Unapproved expansion of scope.

---

## 6. Compliance Monitoring

Compliance should be monitored through proportionate mechanisms such as:

- Operational records.
- Configuration records.
- System logs.
- Human supervision records.
- Mission records.
- Environment monitoring.
- Security monitoring.
- Change records.
- Incident reports.
- Periodic assurance reviews.

Monitoring should support detection without creating unnecessary operational burden.

---

## 7. Compliance Status

A capability may be assessed as:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Compliant** | Operating within applicable authorisation requirements |
| **Conditionally Compliant** | Minor controlled deviation with approved conditions |
| **Non-Compliant** | One or more applicable requirements are not satisfied |
| **Unknown** | Compliance cannot currently be demonstrated |
| **Suspended** | Operational authority has been temporarily withdrawn |
| **Revoked** | Operational authority has been formally withdrawn |

Unknown compliance should not automatically be treated as compliant.

---

## 8. Types of Non-Compliance

Non-compliance may arise from:

### 8.1 Administrative Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Missing records.
- Incorrect documentation.
- Expired administrative approvals.

### 8.2 Operational Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Operating outside mission scope.
- Exceeding environmental boundaries.
- Failure to follow operating conditions.

### 8.3 Technical Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Unauthorised configuration.
- Unapproved software or model change.
- Disabled mandatory control.

### 8.4 Human-Control Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Required supervision not maintained.
- Override capability unavailable.
- Decision authority unclear.

### 8.5 Security Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Unauthorised access.
- Security controls bypassed.
- Compromised dependencies.

### 8.6 Assurance Non-Compliance

Examples:

- Required testing not completed.
- Evidence expired or invalidated.
- Material change introduced without revalidation.

---

## 9. Severity of Non-Compliance

Organisations should classify non-compliance proportionately.

A generic scale is:

- **N1 — Administrative:** negligible operational consequence.
- **N2 — Controlled:** limited deviation with no material effect on authorised risk.
- **N3 — Significant:** deviation that may affect assurance, control or mission risk.
- **N4 — Critical:** deviation capable of creating unacceptable operational, safety, security or legal risk.

N3 and N4 events should normally trigger formal assessment and escalation.

---

## 10. Immediate Protective Action

Where non-compliance may create immediate unacceptable risk, authorised personnel should be able to:

- Stop the affected function.
- Reduce autonomy.
- Transfer control to a human.
- Move the capability to a safe state.
- Restrict operational scope.
- Suspend employment.

Immediate protective action should not be delayed by administrative reporting requirements.

---

## 11. Reporting

Material non-compliance should be reported to the appropriate authority.

Reports should identify, where practicable:

- Capability.
- Mission.
- Environment.
- Configuration.
- Autonomy.
- Human authority.
- Requirement or condition breached.
- Time and circumstances.
- Operational effect.
- Immediate controls.
- Evidence available.
- Recommended action.

---

## 12. Investigation

Significant non-compliance should be investigated to determine:

- What occurred.
- Why it occurred.
- Whether the deviation was intentional or accidental.
- Whether controls failed.
- Whether personnel understood the requirement.
- Whether the system contributed to the deviation.
- Whether similar capabilities may be affected.
- Whether existing assurance remains valid.

Investigation should be proportionate to consequence.

---

## 13. Corrective Action

Corrective actions may include:

- Training.
- Procedural changes.
- Technical controls.
- Configuration correction.
- Increased supervision.
- Reduced autonomy.
- Additional testing.
- Security remediation.
- Environmental restrictions.
- Revised conditions.
- Revalidation.
- Reauthorisation.
- Suspension or revocation.

Corrective action should address the underlying cause rather than only the immediate symptom.

---

## 14. Preventive Action

Where an event identifies a systemic weakness, preventive action should consider:

- Other AI capabilities.
- Shared models.
- Common software.
- Shared suppliers.
- Common data sources.
- Common operational procedures.
- Similar environments.
- Common human-control arrangements.

A local compliance failure may reveal a broader governance weakness.

---

## 15. Enforcement

Enforcement should be:

- Proportionate.
- Evidence-based.
- Consistent.
- Accountable.
- Traceable.
- Appropriate to the severity of the deviation.

Possible governance responses include:

1. Record and close.
2. Corrective action.
3. Additional monitoring.
4. Additional assurance.
5. Restriction.
6. Suspension.
7. Revalidation.
8. Reauthorisation.
9. Revocation.

D-AIGAAF does not prescribe personnel disciplinary measures; those remain governed by applicable organisational rules and law.

---

## 16. Deliberate Circumvention

Intentional circumvention of authorisation controls should receive heightened scrutiny.

Examples include:

- Deliberately bypassing mandatory safeguards.
- Concealing a material system change.
- Operating outside authorised autonomy.
- Suppressing evidence of failure.
- Misrepresenting assurance status.
- Deliberately disabling monitoring or control mechanisms.

Such cases should be escalated under applicable governance, legal and disciplinary processes.

---

## 17. AI-System Detection of Non-Compliance

AI systems may assist in detecting:

- Configuration deviations.
- Unexpected operating conditions.
- Boundary violations.
- Missing supervision.
- Anomalous behaviour.
- Expired authorisation.
- Control failures.

However:

**Detection by AI does not itself constitute enforcement authority.**

Human governance remains responsible for consequential enforcement decisions.

---

## 18. Non-Compliance During Degraded or Disconnected Operations

When communications or monitoring are degraded:

- Existing authority remains in force unless emergency procedures specify otherwise.
- Loss of connectivity does not create additional AI authority.
- Local protective procedures should apply.
- Available evidence should be preserved.
- Reporting should occur when communications are restored.
- Post-event review should determine whether authorisation remains valid.

---

## 19. Relationship with Suspension and Revocation

Non-compliance may trigger:

**Deviation → Assessment → Restriction / Corrective Action → Revalidation / Reauthorisation**

For serious cases:

**Critical Deviation → Immediate Protective Action → Suspension → Investigation → Assurance Review → Restoration / Reauthorisation / Revocation**

Not every deviation requires suspension, but no material deviation should be ignored.

---

## 20. Compliance and Change Control

A change introduced without the required governance process may itself constitute non-compliance.

Examples include:

- Model updates.
- Software updates.
- Data changes.
- Hardware changes.
- Configuration changes.
- Autonomy changes.
- New operating environments.
- New mission uses.
- Changes to human supervision.

The applicable change-control process should determine whether revalidation or reauthorisation is required.

---

## 21. Compliance Evidence

Evidence should be sufficient to demonstrate:

- What was authorised.
- What conditions applied.
- What configuration was used.
- What actually occurred.
- Who exercised authority.
- What controls were active.
- What deviation occurred, if any.
- What action followed.

Where evidence is unavailable, the organisation should assess whether assurance and authorisation remain defensible.

---

## 22. Compliance Register

A Compliance Register may capture:

- Compliance identifier.
- Capability.
- Authorisation identifier.
- Requirement or condition.
- Compliance status.
- Evidence.
- Deviation.
- Severity.
- Responsible authority.
- Immediate action.
- Corrective action.
- Due date.
- Verification.
- Closure.
- Related incident/change/revalidation records.

---

## 23. Closure

A compliance issue should be closed only when:

- The deviation has been assessed.
- Required corrective action is complete.
- Effectiveness has been verified.
- Residual risk is understood.
- Required assurance has been restored.
- Authorisation status has been confirmed.

Closure should not be based solely on completion of an administrative action.

---

## 24. Governance Review

Material compliance trends should be reviewed to identify:

- Repeated deviations.
- Weak controls.
- Poorly understood conditions.
- Unrealistic operating boundaries.
- Training gaps.
- Technical weaknesses.
- Supplier issues.
- Emerging risks.

Recurring non-compliance may indicate that the underlying authorisation or governance design needs revision.

---

## 25. Golden Thread

Compliance preserves the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Reauthorisation**

A compliance event should be traceable to the requirement, control, evidence and authority that established the relevant operational boundary.

---

## 26. Core Rule

**No Defence AI capability should be treated as compliant merely because it remains operational. Compliance requires demonstrable adherence to the authorised mission, environment, autonomy, human authority, configuration, conditions and assurance basis. Material non-compliance shall trigger proportionate corrective action and, where necessary, restriction, suspension, revalidation or reauthorisation.**
