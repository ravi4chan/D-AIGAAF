# 14 Authorisation Dispute and Review

## 1. Purpose

This document defines governance for resolving disagreements, challenges and formal reviews concerning Defence AI operational authorisation decisions.

The objective is to ensure that disagreement about authority, risk, assurance, autonomy, human control or operational employment can be addressed without creating ambiguity or uncontrolled AI use.

---

## 2. Core Principle

**A disagreement about an operational authorisation decision shall not create implied authority to proceed. Where authority, risk or compliance is materially disputed, the issue shall be resolved or escalated through a defined governance process.**

---

## 3. Scope

This process may apply to disputes concerning:

- Grant of authorisation.
- Conditions and restrictions.
- Scope.
- Autonomy level.
- Human decision rights.
- Residual risk.
- Assurance evidence.
- TEVV findings.
- Operational environment.
- Security concerns.
- Data integrity.
- Configuration.
- Exceptions and derogations.
- Suspension or revocation.
- Revalidation.
- Reauthorisation.
- Compliance findings.

---

## 4. Types of Disagreement

Disagreement may be:

### 4.1 Technical

Concern about:

- Model performance.
- System behaviour.
- Reliability.
- Security.
- Configuration.
- Data.
- Dependencies.

### 4.2 Assurance

Concern about:

- Sufficiency of evidence.
- Test coverage.
- Independence.
- Evidence currency.
- Unresolved findings.
- Applicability of previous testing.

### 4.3 Operational

Concern about:

- Mission suitability.
- Environmental assumptions.
- Operational boundaries.
- Human workload.
- Practical controllability.
- Mission effectiveness.

### 4.4 Governance

Concern about:

- Authority.
- Delegation.
- Conditions.
- Risk acceptance.
- Scope.
- Compliance.

### 4.5 Legal or Policy

Concern that employment may conflict with:

- Applicable law.
- Policy.
- Rules.
- Mandates.
- Binding organisational requirements.

---

## 5. Right to Raise a Concern

Relevant personnel should be able to raise a good-faith concern regarding an AI capability without being required to accept an authorisation decision they reasonably believe may create unacceptable risk.

This should include:

- Operators.
- Commanders.
- Technical personnel.
- Assurance personnel.
- Security personnel.
- OAIA.
- Risk owners.
- System managers.
- Other designated stakeholders.

---

## 6. Immediate Safety and Control

A dispute process shall not prevent immediate protective action.

Where a credible concern indicates potential unacceptable harm, authorised personnel may:

- Stop an affected function.
- Reduce autonomy.
- Transfer control to a human.
- Restrict employment.
- Move to a safe state.
- Request suspension.

Immediate protective action should be treated separately from final resolution of the dispute.

---

## 7. No Retaliation for Good-Faith Concerns

Organisations should provide appropriate protection for personnel who raise legitimate safety, security, assurance or governance concerns in good faith.

The objective is to surface risk rather than suppress disagreement.

---

## 8. Informal Resolution

Where appropriate, disagreements should first be clarified through discussion between relevant authorities.

The discussion should establish:

- Issue.
- Applicable authorisation.
- Evidence.
- Relevant requirement.
- Risk.
- Operational context.
- Decision right.
- Proposed resolution.

Informal resolution should not be used to bypass formal escalation where the issue is material.

---

## 9. Formal Review Request

A formal review request should identify:

- Authorisation identifier.
- Capability.
- Decision being challenged.
- Grounds for challenge.
- Evidence.
- Relevant conditions.
- Risk implications.
- Requested action.
- Requesting authority or individual.
- Date.

---

## 10. Grounds for Review

A review may be justified where:

- Material evidence was overlooked.
- New evidence has emerged.
- Risk was incorrectly assessed.
- Assurance evidence is insufficient.
- A condition is unclear or impractical.
- The operating environment has changed.
- Human control is inadequate.
- Autonomy has changed.
- Configuration has changed.
- A security concern exists.
- The authorisation exceeds the authority of the decision maker.
- A legal or policy requirement has changed.
- A material compliance issue exists.

---

## 11. Review Authority

The review should be conducted by an authority with sufficient independence and competence.

Where practicable, the reviewer should not be the sole person responsible for the original disputed decision.

The required level of independence should be proportionate to:

- Consequence.
- Autonomy.
- Risk.
- Materiality of the dispute.
- Potential operational impact.

---

## 12. Review Evidence

The reviewer should consider relevant evidence including:

- Operational Authorisation Record.
- Defence AI Assurance Record.
- TEVV evidence.
- Risk assessments.
- Autonomy assessment.
- Human-control assessment.
- Environmental assessment.
- Security findings.
- Data assurance.
- Configuration records.
- Incident records.
- Change records.
- Compliance findings.
- Lessons learned.
- Applicable legal and policy requirements.

---

## 13. Review Outcomes

A review may result in:

1. **R1 — Decision Confirmed**
2. **R2 — Decision Confirmed with Revised Conditions**
3. **R3 — Additional Evidence Required**
4. **R4 — Additional Assurance Required**
5. **R5 — Revalidation Required**
6. **R6 — Reauthorisation Required**
7. **R7 — Scope Reduced or Restricted**
8. **R8 — Authorisation Suspended**
9. **R9 — Authorisation Revoked**

The outcome should include a clear rationale.

---

## 14. Disputed Authorisation Pending Review

Unless an authorised emergency or protective procedure provides otherwise:

- A disputed authorisation should not automatically expand in scope.
- Existing restrictions remain applicable.
- A material unresolved safety or control concern may justify restriction or suspension.
- The capability should not operate outside the existing authorisation merely because review is pending.

---

## 15. Disagreement Between Human Authority and AI

AI output does not constitute a governance appeal.

Where an AI recommendation conflicts with a human decision:

- The authorised human decision maker retains authority.
- The AI cannot override the decision.
- The disagreement may be recorded where consequential.
- Material uncertainty should be escalated where appropriate.

---

## 16. Technical and Operational Disagreement

Where technical personnel conclude that a capability is unsafe or insufficiently assured while operational personnel consider it necessary, the disagreement should be explicitly documented.

The final decision should identify:

- Evidence.
- Risk.
- Authority.
- Conditions.
- Residual uncertainty.
- Decision rationale.

No party should resolve the disagreement merely by asserting rank, urgency or technical confidence.

---

## 17. Risk Acceptance Disputes

Where disagreement concerns residual risk:

- The applicable risk owner should be identified.
- The risk assessment should be reviewed.
- Evidence should be examined.
- Controls should be reconsidered.
- The appropriate risk acceptance authority should be identified.

A person should not accept risk merely because another stakeholder has requested operational use.

---

## 18. Autonomy Disputes

Where there is disagreement about the appropriate autonomy level:

- The actual system behaviour should be established.
- The proposed autonomy should be compared with the authorised autonomy.
- Human control should be assessed.
- Consequence should be considered.
- Additional TEVV may be required.
- Higher authority should be engaged where required.

Higher autonomy should not be assumed merely because the capability can technically perform an action.

---

## 19. Human-Control Disputes

Where meaningful human control is disputed, the organisation should assess:

- Who has authority.
- Whether the person can understand the situation.
- Whether the person can intervene.
- Whether intervention is timely.
- Whether the person can override or stop the capability.
- Whether workload is manageable.
- Whether communications are available.
- Whether the system communicates relevant uncertainty.

Where meaningful control cannot be demonstrated for the authorised use, restriction or suspension should be considered.

---

## 20. Security Disputes

A credible security concern may require immediate protective action.

Potential responses include:

- Restriction.
- Isolation.
- Reduced functionality.
- Increased monitoring.
- Suspension.
- Security investigation.
- Revalidation.
- Reauthorisation.

Operational urgency should not automatically override a material security control concern.

---

## 21. Emergency Dispute Resolution

During emergencies:

1. Protect people and assets.
2. Preserve human authority.
3. Apply pre-authorised contingency procedures.
4. Use the minimum necessary operational authority.
5. Escalate as soon as practicable.
6. Record the decision and circumstances.
7. Conduct post-event review.

Emergency circumstances may change how quickly a decision is made, but should not eliminate accountability.

---

## 22. Escalation

If a dispute cannot be resolved at the current level, it should escalate according to defined decision rights.

A generic path is:

**Operator / User → System Manager → OAIA → Operational / Command Authority → Authorising Authority**

Technical, assurance, security and legal matters should also be escalated through their respective authorities where appropriate.

---

## 23. Deadlock

Where authorities remain divided after escalation:

- The issue should be referred to the designated higher authority.
- The existing authorisation and restrictions should remain in force unless lawfully changed.
- No participant should assume additional authority because consensus was not reached.
- For high-consequence unresolved matters, restriction or suspension should be considered.

---

## 24. Review Timeframes

Organisations should establish proportionate review timeframes.

Higher priority should be given to disputes involving:

- Immediate safety risk.
- Loss of human control.
- Unauthorised autonomy.
- Security compromise.
- Critical mission dependency.
- Material legal or policy concern.

Time pressure should not be used to suppress material evidence.

---

## 25. Records

Material disputes and reviews should be recorded.

The record should include:

- Issue.
- Parties involved.
- Authorisation.
- Evidence considered.
- Risk.
- Decision rights.
- Arguments or concerns.
- Review findings.
- Decision.
- Conditions.
- Follow-up actions.
- Date.
- Responsible authority.

---

## 26. Review of the Review Process

Recurring disputes should be analysed for systemic weaknesses, such as:

- Ambiguous decision rights.
- Poorly defined authorisation conditions.
- Insufficient evidence.
- Inadequate human-AI interfaces.
- Unrealistic operating boundaries.
- Weak escalation mechanisms.
- Training deficiencies.
- Governance gaps.

The dispute process itself should therefore contribute to continuous assurance.

---

## 27. Golden Thread

Dispute and review processes preserve the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Reauthorisation**

A material challenge should be traceable to the requirement, evidence, risk and authority underlying the disputed decision.

---

## 28. Core Rule

**Disagreement is a governance signal, not an operational permission. A material dispute concerning risk, assurance, autonomy, human control, security or authority shall be resolved or escalated through defined decision rights, while existing operational boundaries remain enforceable and immediate protective action remains available.**
