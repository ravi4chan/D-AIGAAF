# 44 — Authorisation Organisational Levels and Scope

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how D-AIGAAF allocates and coordinates operational authorisation across different organisational levels.

Defence AI capabilities may be developed, managed and employed across strategic, operational, tactical, technical and support organisations. The authority to approve, constrain, monitor or withdraw AI employment must therefore be clearly aligned with the level of consequence and the scope of the decision.

---

## 2. Core Principle

> **Operational authorisation shall be exercised at an organisational level appropriate to the consequence, autonomy, risk and scope of the proposed AI employment, with clear boundaries between strategic governance, operational command, technical authority and system-level control.**

No organisational level should exercise authority beyond its defined mandate.

---

## 3. Organisational Authority Model

D-AIGAAF uses a conceptual hierarchy:

**Strategic Governance → Operational Command → Formation/Unit Authority → System/Capability Authority → Operator/User**

The exact organisational structure will vary between adopting organisations.

The framework should map these levels to applicable national and defence command arrangements.

---

## 4. Strategic Governance

Strategic governance establishes:

- AI policy;
- organisational risk appetite;
- governance principles;
- legal and policy alignment;
- high-level autonomy policy;
- portfolio priorities;
- strategic assurance requirements;
- organisational authority limits.

Strategic governance should not normally substitute for mission-level operational decisions unless explicitly authorised to do so.

---

## 5. Operational Command Authority

Operational command may determine:

- mission requirements;
- operational priorities;
- employment context;
- operational objectives;
- authorised use within delegated authority;
- operational restrictions;
- escalation.

Command authority remains subject to higher-level policy, law, authorisation constraints and applicable risk acceptance requirements.

---

## 6. Formation and Unit-Level Authority

Where authority is delegated to formations, units or equivalent organisations, the delegation should define:

- scope;
- mission;
- environment;
- autonomy;
- duration;
- configuration;
- human authority;
- conditions;
- restrictions;
- escalation thresholds.

Delegated authority should not be interpreted as unrestricted authority over every AI capability available to the organisation.

---

## 7. System and Capability Authority

System-level authority may cover:

- technical configuration;
- maintenance;
- system readiness;
- technical restrictions;
- security controls;
- system availability;
- technical recovery.

Technical authority does not automatically confer authority to employ the system operationally.

---

## 8. Operator and User Authority

Operators should have clearly defined authority to:

- use authorised functions;
- review AI outputs;
- reject recommendations;
- request clarification;
- intervene where authorised;
- activate defined protective measures;
- escalate concerns.

Operators should not be expected to interpret ambiguous organisational authority during consequential activity.

---

## 9. Authorising Authority

The organisation should designate an **Authorising Authority** appropriate to:

- consequence;
- autonomy;
- mission criticality;
- operational scope;
- risk;
- organisational authority.

The Authorising Authority grants, conditions, restricts, suspends or revokes operational authority within its mandate.

---

## 10. Authority versus Responsibility

D-AIGAAF distinguishes:

**Authority** — the right to make or approve a defined decision.

**Responsibility** — the duty to perform or manage an assigned function.

**Accountability** — the obligation to answer for the exercise of authority and responsibility.

These concepts should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

## 11. Delegated Authority

Delegated operational authority should be:

- explicit;
- bounded;
- documented;
- proportionate;
- competence-based;
- time-limited where appropriate;
- reviewable;
- revocable.

A delegate cannot exercise authority greater than that possessed by the delegating authority.

---

## 12. Authority Scope

Every delegated or assigned authority should specify:

- capability;
- mission;
- environment;
- autonomy;
- human authority;
- configuration;
- conditions;
- geographic scope;
- temporal scope;
- consequence level;
- escalation requirements.

This creates a controlled authority boundary.

---

## 13. High-Consequence Authority

Capabilities capable of materially affecting:

- human life;
- physical safety;
- critical infrastructure;
- significant property;
- strategic outcomes;

should require authority at an organisational level appropriate to their consequence.

The precise level should be determined by applicable law, doctrine, policy and organisational governance.

---

## 14. Autonomy and Organisational Level

Higher autonomy should generally require stronger authority and oversight.

The D-AIGAAF working autonomy model is:

- **A0 — No Meaningful AI Decision**
- **A1 — Information / Observation**
- **A2 — Analysis / Recommendation**
- **A3 — Human-Authorised Action**
- **A4 — Supervised Autonomous Action**
- **A5 — Independent Consequential Autonomy**

This is a working construct and should be mapped to applicable national, defence, legal, doctrinal and international terminology.

---

## 15. Cross-Level Authority Conflicts

Conflicts may occur between:

- strategic policy;
- operational command;
- unit authority;
- technical authority;
- security authority;
- risk authority;
- AI recommendations.

Such conflicts should be resolved using the precedence and conflict-resolution provisions of D-AIGAAF.

An AI system should never independently determine which organisational authority has precedence where human authority is unclear.

---

## 16. Technical Authority and Operational Command

Technical and operational authorities have different responsibilities.

### Technical Authority

Determines matters such as:

- system integrity;
- configuration;
- technical limitations;
- maintenance;
- technical readiness.

### Operational Authority

Determines matters such as:

- mission employment;
- operational priorities;
- operational restrictions;
- human decision authority.

Neither should silently override the legitimate authority of the other.

---

## 17. Security Authority

Security authorities may impose restrictions where:

- compromise is suspected;
- integrity is uncertain;
- security controls fail;
- threat conditions change;
- configuration cannot be trusted.

Security restrictions should be communicated to the appropriate operational authority.

---

## 18. Risk Owner

The Risk Owner should be responsible for:

- identifying material risk;
- monitoring risk;
- ensuring treatment;
- determining whether residual risk remains acceptable within their authority;
- escalating risk beyond delegated thresholds.

Risk ownership does not automatically confer operational authorisation.

---

## 19. Operational AI Advisor

The **Operational AI Advisor (OAIA)** supports command and governance by connecting:

**Mission ↔ AI Capability ↔ Risk ↔ Assurance ↔ Human Authority**

The OAIA may advise on:

- AI limitations;
- assurance evidence;
- autonomy;
- human control;
- operational suitability;
- uncertainty;
- emerging AI behaviour.

The OAIA should not become an implicit substitute for the designated command or authorising authority.

---

## 20. Authority During Degraded Operations

When normal command or communication arrangements are degraded:

- existing authority remains bounded;
- predefined delegated authority applies;
- autonomy should not expand automatically;
- protective actions may be taken where pre-authorised;
- unresolved matters should be escalated when practicable;
- decisions should be recorded.

Disconnection does not create new AI authority.

---

## 21. Authority During Emergency Operations

Emergency conditions may require accelerated decision-making.

Pre-authorised emergency authorities should define:

- who may act;
- what actions are permitted;
- what limitations apply;
- when escalation is required;
- when the capability must enter a safe or restricted state.

Emergency authority should not become permanent operational authority without appropriate governance.

---

## 22. Authority Transfer

When responsibility changes between:

- commanders;
- shifts;
- units;
- organisations;
- operators;

the transfer should be explicit and recorded.

The receiving authority should understand:

- current authorisation;
- restrictions;
- configuration;
- environment;
- autonomy;
- risks;
- incidents;
- monitoring status.

---

## 23. Authority Expansion

An authority holder should not assume that authority over one capability, mission or environment automatically extends to:

- another capability;
- another mission;
- another environment;
- higher autonomy;
- additional users;
- different configuration;
- greater consequence.

Material expansion should follow the applicable scope-expansion and reauthorisation process.

---

## 24. Authority Reduction

Authority may be reduced through:

- restriction;
- reduced autonomy;
- narrower mission;
- narrower environment;
- additional supervision;
- shorter validity;
- additional conditions.

Reduction may be appropriate where risk increases but complete suspension is not necessary.

---

## 25. Authority Suspension and Revocation

Where the basis for authority is materially compromised, the appropriate authority may:

- restrict;
- suspend;
- revoke.

Examples include:

- serious incident;
- loss of human control;
- material security compromise;
- configuration uncertainty;
- boundary violation;
- unacceptable performance;
- failed mandatory conditions.

The authority to restore should be clearly defined.

---

## 26. Organisational Authority Matrix

A controlled **Organisational AI Authority Matrix** should identify, where applicable:

| Decision | Strategic | Operational | Unit | Technical | Operator |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI policy | Approve | Consult | Inform | Consult | Inform |
| Mission use | Govern | Approve | Execute within authority | Consult | Execute |
| Technical configuration | Govern | Consult | Manage within authority | Approve | Operate |
| Autonomy level | Govern | Approve within limits | Apply within authority | Assess | Operate |
| Risk acceptance | Define framework | Accept within authority | Accept within delegation | Advise | Escalate |
| Operational authorisation | Govern framework | Grant within mandate | Grant if delegated | Advise | No |
| Suspension | May direct | May direct | May act if delegated | Technical restriction | Protective action if authorised |
| Reauthorisation | Govern | Decide within mandate | Recommend | Assure | No |

The table is illustrative and must be adapted to the adopting organisation.

---

## 27. Authority Register

A controlled **Operational AI Authority Register** should record:

- authority holder;
- role;
- delegated authority;
- capability scope;
- mission scope;
- autonomy scope;
- geographic scope;
- validity;
- conditions;
- restrictions;
- escalation thresholds;
- delegation source;
- review date.

---

## 28. Competence

Authority should be exercised only by personnel with appropriate competence.

Competence may include:

- operational knowledge;
- AI literacy;
- risk understanding;
- knowledge of authorisation boundaries;
- understanding of human-control requirements;
- ability to interpret uncertainty;
- knowledge of emergency procedures.

Higher-consequence authority should require proportionately stronger competence.

---

## 29. Governance Review

The organisation should periodically review whether:

- authority is assigned at the correct level;
- delegations remain valid;
- decision rights are clear;
- high-consequence decisions receive appropriate oversight;
- authority conflicts are recurring;
- competence remains adequate;
- organisational changes have altered decision rights.

---

## 30. Governance Questions

The organisation should be able to answer:

1. Who can authorise a Defence AI capability?
2. At what organisational level?
3. Who can accept the associated residual risk?
4. Who can change the autonomy level?
5. Who can suspend or revoke authority?
6. What can be delegated?
7. What cannot be delegated?
8. How are authority boundaries recorded?
9. What happens when command and technical authorities disagree?
10. What happens when organisational structure changes?
11. Who retains accountability after delegation?
12. How does authority transfer during shifts or organisational handovers?

---

## 31. Golden Thread

Organisational authority should remain connected to the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Escalation → Change/Incident → Revalidation/Reauthorisation**

This ensures that organisational decision rights remain connected to the evidence and operational conditions supporting them.

---

## 32. Core Rule

> **Defence AI operational authority shall be exercised at an organisational level appropriate to the consequence, autonomy, risk and scope of employment. Strategic governance, operational command, technical authority, risk ownership, security authority and system operation shall have clearly defined decision rights. Delegation shall be explicit and bounded, and no organisational level, operator or AI system shall acquire authority beyond its defined mandate merely because circumstances become urgent, communications are degraded or the capability is technically able to perform an action.**
