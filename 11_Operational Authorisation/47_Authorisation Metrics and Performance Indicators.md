# 47 — Authorisation Metrics and Performance Indicators

## 1. Purpose

This document defines a measurement framework for determining whether the D-AIGAAF operational authorisation regime is functioning effectively.

The purpose of metrics is not to reduce governance to a numerical score. Metrics should provide evidence about the quality, timeliness, effectiveness and resilience of authorisation decisions and identify areas requiring corrective action.

---

## 2. Core Principle

> **D-AIGAAF metrics shall measure whether operational authority is being granted, exercised, monitored, reviewed and withdrawn in a controlled, evidence-based and accountable manner—not merely how many AI capabilities have been authorised.**

---

## 3. What Should Be Measured

The authorisation regime should measure:

- authority quality;
- evidence sufficiency;
- risk management;
- human control;
- autonomy governance;
- operational readiness;
- boundary compliance;
- monitoring;
- incidents;
- change management;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation;
- suspension and withdrawal;
- organisational competence;
- auditability.

---

## 4. Metrics versus Performance Indicators

A **Metric** is a measurable value.

A **Performance Indicator** is a metric interpreted against a governance objective, threshold or trend.

A single metric should not normally determine an authorisation decision.

---

## 5. Measurement Principles

Metrics should be:

- relevant;
- measurable;
- traceable;
- proportionate;
- timely;
- comparable where appropriate;
- resistant to gaming;
- linked to decisions;
- reviewed periodically.

Metrics should support judgement rather than replace it.

---

## 6. Authorisation Portfolio Indicators

The organisation may track:

- total capabilities in the register;
- authorised capabilities;
- conditionally authorised capabilities;
- restricted capabilities;
- suspended capabilities;
- revoked capabilities;
- pending authorisations;
- expired authorisations;
- overdue reviews.

These indicators provide portfolio visibility but should not be treated as measures of governance quality by themselves.

---

## 7. Evidence Sufficiency Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- percentage of authorisations with complete evidence packages;
- number of unresolved material evidence gaps;
- number of contradictory findings;
- age of critical evidence;
- percentage of evidence independently reviewed;
- evidence traceability to requirements;
- evidence traceability to operational conditions.

A high volume of evidence does not necessarily indicate high assurance.

---

## 8. Risk Indicators

Risk-related indicators may include:

- number of high-consequence capabilities;
- residual-risk ratings;
- overdue risk reviews;
- unresolved high-severity risks;
- risk acceptance exceptions;
- recurring risk categories;
- risk escalation frequency;
- changes in risk profile over time.

Trends should be examined rather than isolated values.

---

## 9. Autonomy Indicators

The organisation may track the distribution of capabilities across:

- A0 — No Meaningful AI Decision;
- A1 — Information / Observation;
- A2 — Analysis / Recommendation;
- A3 — Human-Authorised Action;
- A4 — Supervised Autonomous Action;
- A5 — Independent Consequential Autonomy.

Additional indicators may include:

- autonomy increases over time;
- autonomy changes requiring reauthorisation;
- unauthorised autonomy transitions;
- autonomy-related incidents;
- autonomy restrictions;
- failed intervention or override events.

These are working D-AIGAAF constructs and should be mapped to applicable terminology.

---

## 10. Human-Control Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- percentage of applicable systems with defined human decision rights;
- human-control assessment results;
- intervention success rate;
- override availability;
- failed intervention events;
- human workload findings;
- automation-bias findings;
- training/competence gaps;
- incidents involving loss or degradation of human control.

Human-control metrics should reflect operational reality rather than documentation alone.

---

## 11. Operational Readiness Indicators

The organisation may measure:

- readiness assessment completion;
- readiness failures;
- conditional readiness;
- readiness expiry;
- failed entry criteria;
- deployment delays caused by assurance gaps;
- recurring readiness deficiencies;
- percentage of deployments with complete readiness records.

---

## 12. Boundary Compliance Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- boundary violations;
- attempted prohibited functions;
- operation outside authorised environment;
- operation outside authorised autonomy;
- configuration outside baseline;
- unauthorised mission expansion;
- unauthorised user access;
- time or geographic scope violations.

Boundary events should be investigated according to consequence.

---

## 13. Monitoring Indicators

Monitoring may measure:

- monitoring coverage;
- monitoring availability;
- threshold breaches;
- false alerts;
- missed alerts;
- unresolved alerts;
- time to detection;
- time to human notification;
- time to protective action;
- monitoring gaps during degraded operations.

Monitoring effectiveness should be assessed, not merely monitoring presence.

---

## 14. Incident Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- number of AI-related incidents;
- severity;
- recurrence;
- loss of human control;
- unexpected behaviour;
- boundary violations;
- security incidents;
- data integrity incidents;
- fail-safe events;
- time to containment;
- time to investigation;
- corrective-action closure.

Incident counts should be interpreted carefully. Increased reporting can indicate improved governance rather than deteriorating performance.

---

## 15. Change-Control Indicators

The organisation may measure:

- number of AI changes;
- percentage classified as material/non-material;
- changes requiring additional TEVV;
- changes requiring revalidation;
- changes requiring reauthorisation;
- unauthorised changes;
- failed change assessments;
- rollback events;
- configuration discrepancies.

Special attention should be given to material model or hidden-layer changes.

---

## 16. Revalidation Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- revalidation completion rate;
- overdue revalidation;
- revalidation failures;
- revalidation triggered by incidents;
- revalidation triggered by environmental change;
- revalidation triggered by model changes;
- findings requiring restriction;
- findings requiring reauthorisation.

---

## 17. Reauthorisation Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- scheduled reauthorisations completed on time;
- material changes requiring reauthorisation;
- reauthorisation delays;
- reauthorisation refusals;
- conditional reauthorisations;
- authority reductions;
- reauthorisation following incidents.

A refusal to reauthorise may indicate effective governance rather than governance failure.

---

## 18. Suspension and Revocation Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- number of suspensions;
- number of revocations;
- time from trigger to restriction;
- time from trigger to suspension;
- duration of suspension;
- restoration rate;
- repeated suspension;
- capabilities withdrawn permanently.

The organisation should examine whether suspension mechanisms are actually used when required.

---

## 19. Escalation Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- number of escalations;
- escalation severity;
- escalation response time;
- unresolved escalations;
- repeated escalation categories;
- authority ambiguities;
- escalation failures during degraded operations.

Repeated escalation in the same category may indicate a structural governance problem.

---

## 20. Decision-Quality Indicators

Authorisation decision quality may be assessed against:

- clarity of decision;
- adequacy of evidence;
- risk assessment quality;
- uncertainty visibility;
- autonomy assessment;
- human-control assessment;
- boundary definition;
- conditions;
- independent challenge;
- traceability;
- decision rationale.

D-AIGAAF may use the working decision-quality scale:

- **DQ1 — Strong**
- **DQ2 — Adequate with Conditions**
- **DQ3 — Improvement Required**
- **DQ4 — Insufficient**
- **DQ5 — Unreliable**

---

## 21. Governance Timeliness

Potential indicators include:

- time to complete authorisation;
- time to resolve evidence gaps;
- time to review incidents;
- time to complete revalidation;
- time to reauthorise after material change;
- time to communicate restrictions;
- time to suspend following a qualifying trigger.

Speed should not be optimised at the expense of assurance quality.

---

## 22. Assurance Currency

The organisation should monitor:

- age of critical TEVV evidence;
- age of risk assessments;
- age of environmental assessments;
- age of security assessments;
- age of human-control assessments;
- age of configuration verification;
- overdue assurance activities.

Evidence currency should be assessed relative to system volatility and operational consequence.

---

## 23. Auditability Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- percentage of capabilities with complete authorisation records;
- decision traceability;
- configuration traceability;
- evidence provenance;
- decision-log integrity;
- unresolved audit findings;
- repeat audit findings;
- corrective-action closure.

---

## 24. Organisational Competence Indicators

The organisation may measure:

- authorising personnel trained;
- OAIA competence;
- operator AI literacy;
- technical authority competence;
- assurance personnel competence;
- exercise completion;
- recurring competence gaps;
- overdue training.

Competence should be demonstrated where required, not inferred from appointment alone.

---

## 25. Dependency and Supply-Chain Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- critical dependencies;
- single points of failure;
- supplier concentration;
- critical external services;
- unresolved supplier assurance findings;
- dependency changes;
- unapproved external updates;
- dependency-related incidents.

These indicators should be connected to continuity and resilience governance.

---

## 26. Security Indicators

Security indicators may include:

- AI security incidents;
- model integrity events;
- data integrity events;
- unauthorised configuration changes;
- adversarial evaluation findings;
- unresolved vulnerabilities;
- security exceptions;
- time to containment.

Security metrics should be interpreted together with operational consequence.

---

## 27. Environment Indicators

The organisation may track:

- operations within demonstrated envelope;
- operations near boundaries;
- operations outside envelope;
- environmental change events;
- environmental assumption failures;
- degraded/disconnected events;
- adversarial-environment findings;
- environment-related restrictions.

---

## 28. Fail-Safe Indicators

Potential indicators include:

- fail-safe tests completed;
- fail-safe test failures;
- intervention failures;
- safe-state transition failures;
- unexpected autonomy transitions;
- recovery failures;
- time to safe state.

A fail-safe should be measured by demonstrated performance, not simply by its documented existence.

---

## 29. Portfolio and Common-Mode Indicators

Portfolio governance should examine:

- shared models;
- shared datasets;
- shared suppliers;
- shared infrastructure;
- common software components;
- common dependencies;
- repeated failure modes;
- concentration of high autonomy;
- concentration of high-consequence capabilities.

Common-mode risk can remain hidden when systems are assessed only individually.

---

## 30. Metric Thresholds

Metrics may use working states:

### Green — Within Expected Range

No material governance concern identified.

### Amber — Watch

Trend or condition requires increased attention.

### Red — Material Concern

Defined corrective action or governance intervention required.

### Grey — Unknown

Insufficient or unreliable information is available.

Grey should not automatically be treated as green.

---

## 31. Leading and Lagging Indicators

### Leading indicators

Examples:

- overdue assurance;
- evidence gaps;
- training deficiencies;
- increasing uncertainty;
- configuration drift;
- repeated boundary warnings;
- dependency concentration.

### Lagging indicators

Examples:

- incidents;
- mission failures;
- safety events;
- security compromise;
- loss of human control;
- suspension or revocation.

A mature governance regime should use both.

---

## 32. Metric Gaming and Perverse Incentives

Metrics can create undesirable behaviour.

Examples include:

- reducing reported incidents to improve statistics;
- avoiding escalation;
- classifying changes as non-material;
- closing findings without resolving root causes;
- prioritising authorisation speed over assurance;
- measuring documentation completion rather than evidence quality.

Governance should therefore assess metric context and qualitative evidence.

---

## 33. Management Dashboard

A D-AIGAAF authorisation dashboard may provide:

**Authority | Risk | Autonomy | Human Control | Readiness | Environment | Security | Evidence | Boundaries | Incidents | Change | Revalidation | Reauthorisation**

The dashboard should support decision-making and escalation.

> **The dashboard informs authority; it does not create authority.**

---

## 34. Metric Ownership

Each material indicator should have:

- metric definition;
- owner;
- data source;
- calculation method;
- reporting frequency;
- threshold;
- interpretation;
- escalation path;
- review authority.

Metrics should be traceable to reliable source records.

---

## 35. Data Quality

Metrics should themselves be subject to assurance.

The organisation should assess:

- completeness;
- accuracy;
- timeliness;
- provenance;
- consistency;
- manipulation risk;
- missing data;
- conflicting records.

Poor metric data should not be treated as evidence of good governance.

---

## 36. Governance Review

Metrics should be reviewed periodically to determine:

- whether they remain relevant;
- whether thresholds remain appropriate;
- whether new risks are emerging;
- whether indicators are producing useful decisions;
- whether metrics are being gamed;
- whether recurring findings require structural change.

---

## 37. Corrective Action

A material adverse trend should trigger proportionate action such as:

**Observe → Investigate → Increase Assurance → Restrict → Revalidate → Reauthorise → Suspend**

Corrective action should address root causes rather than only the metric value.

---

## 38. Continuous Improvement

Metrics should feed the D-AIGAAF continuous assurance cycle:

**Measure → Interpret → Challenge → Act → Verify → Learn → Update**

This ensures that measurement produces governance improvement.

---

## 39. Governance Questions

The organisation should be able to answer:

1. Are authorisation decisions being made at the correct level?
2. Is sufficient evidence available?
3. Are high-consequence systems receiving enhanced governance?
4. Is human control effective in practice?
5. Are autonomy changes being detected and governed?
6. Are boundaries being respected?
7. Are incidents producing corrective action?
8. Are material changes triggering revalidation or reauthorisation?
9. Are authorisations being reviewed on time?
10. Are suspended capabilities prevented from operating?
11. Are common-mode portfolio risks visible?
12. Are metrics themselves reliable?
13. Are metrics driving the right behaviour?

---

## 40. Core Rule

> **D-AIGAAF shall use metrics and performance indicators to assess the effectiveness, quality, timeliness and resilience of the Defence AI operational authorisation regime. Metrics shall support human judgement rather than replace it, and shall measure evidence quality, risk, autonomy, human control, readiness, boundaries, monitoring, incidents, change, revalidation, reauthorisation, suspension, competence and auditability. No single metric, dashboard status or numerical score shall create or extend operational authority. Material adverse trends, unreliable evidence or systemic governance weaknesses shall trigger proportionate investigation, corrective action, additional assurance, restriction, revalidation, reauthorisation or suspension as required.**
