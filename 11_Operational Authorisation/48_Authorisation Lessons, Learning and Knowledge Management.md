# 48 — Authorisation Lessons, Learning and Knowledge Management

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how D-AIGAAF converts operational experience, incidents, assurance findings, decisions, near misses and emerging evidence into institutional learning.

The objective is to prevent lessons from remaining isolated within individual teams or missions and to ensure that relevant knowledge improves future authorisation, assurance, design, training and operational employment.

---

## 2. Core Principle

> **Operational experience shall be treated as a source of assurance and governance learning. Material lessons shall be captured, assessed, validated and incorporated into relevant D-AIGAAF controls, evidence, training, authorisation conditions and future decisions.**

---

## 3. Scope

Lessons may arise from:

- operational employment;
- incidents;
- near misses;
- unexpected AI behaviour;
- boundary events;
- autonomy transitions;
- loss or degradation of human control;
- security events;
- data-quality failures;
- environmental conditions;
- dependency failures;
- TEVV;
- audits;
- governance reviews;
- exercises;
- training;
- authorisation decisions;
- suspension or revocation;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

---

## 4. Lesson versus Finding

A **Finding** identifies an observed deficiency, deviation, uncertainty or condition requiring attention.

A **Lesson** identifies knowledge gained from experience that may improve future decisions, controls or outcomes.

A lesson may arise from either a successful or unsuccessful outcome.

---

## 5. Learning Cycle

D-AIGAAF uses:

**Observe → Record → Assess → Learn → Act → Verify → Update**

The cycle should connect operational experience to governance change.

---

## 6. Learning Objectives

The organisation should seek to determine:

- what happened;
- why it happened;
- what was expected;
- what was unexpected;
- what assumptions failed;
- what controls worked;
- what controls failed;
- what evidence was missing;
- whether the issue was local or systemic;
- what should change;
- whether the change requires revalidation or reauthorisation.

---

## 7. Lesson Sources

Relevant information may originate from:

- operators;
- commanders;
- OAIA;
- technical teams;
- assurance teams;
- security teams;
- risk owners;
- maintainers;
- developers;
- system managers;
- independent reviewers;
- audit teams;
- external assurance;
- authorised partners.

All sources should be assessed according to reliability and relevance.

---

## 8. Lesson Capture

Material lessons should be recorded with sufficient context to support later analysis.

A Lesson Record should include:

- identifier;
- date;
- source;
- capability;
- mission/use case;
- environment;
- configuration;
- autonomy;
- human-control context;
- event or observation;
- expected behaviour;
- actual behaviour;
- consequence;
- uncertainty;
- initial assessment;
- immediate action;
- proposed lesson;
- owner;
- follow-up;
- status.

---

## 9. Immediate Learning

Where an observation may indicate immediate risk, learning should not wait for a full investigation.

Immediate measures may include:

- increased monitoring;
- restriction;
- reduced autonomy;
- additional supervision;
- suspension;
- configuration control;
- additional testing;
- operational notification.

Protective action should follow existing decision rights.

---

## 10. Lesson Classification

Lessons may be classified according to their significance:

### L1 — Local Improvement

Limited to a specific process, team or context.

### L2 — Capability Improvement

Relevant to a specific AI capability or system.

### L3 — Cross-Capability Learning

Relevant to multiple Defence AI capabilities.

### L4 — Governance Learning

Indicates a weakness or improvement opportunity in D-AIGAAF governance.

### L5 — Strategic Learning

May affect organisational policy, risk appetite, acquisition, doctrine or strategic governance.

Classification should be reviewed as evidence develops.

---

## 11. Lesson Quality

A lesson should distinguish:

**Fact** — what is supported by evidence.

**Interpretation** — what the evidence appears to indicate.

**Judgement** — what should change as a result.

This distinction reduces the risk of converting assumptions into institutional knowledge.

---

## 12. Root-Cause Analysis

Material lessons should consider whether the cause originated in:

- requirements;
- data;
- model development;
- software;
- integration;
- configuration;
- human factors;
- operational environment;
- autonomy;
- security;
- supply chain;
- dependency;
- authorisation;
- training;
- procedures;
- monitoring.

The objective is to identify systemic causes rather than assign blame prematurely.

---

## 13. Unexpected AI Behaviour

Unexpected behaviour should be assessed for:

- reproducibility;
- severity;
- operational relevance;
- possible causes;
- model/configuration relationship;
- environmental influence;
- data influence;
- human interaction;
- security implications;
- potential recurrence.

Unexpected behaviour may require restriction, additional TEVV, revalidation or reauthorisation.

---

## 14. Near Misses

Near misses should be treated as valuable evidence.

A near miss may reveal:

- inadequate boundary controls;
- insufficient human oversight;
- weak monitoring;
- poor uncertainty communication;
- environmental assumptions;
- configuration weaknesses;
- latent system risk.

Absence of harm does not mean absence of a governance problem.

---

## 15. Successful Outcomes

Lessons should also be captured from successful operations.

Examples include:

- effective controls;
- successful intervention;
- useful uncertainty communication;
- successful degraded operation;
- effective fail-safe;
- effective monitoring;
- effective human-AI collaboration.

Success should not automatically prove that the underlying governance is optimal.

---

## 16. Cross-Capability Learning

A lesson from one AI capability should be assessed for relevance to:

- similar models;
- common datasets;
- shared suppliers;
- common software;
- similar environments;
- common autonomy patterns;
- shared human interfaces;
- related missions.

This helps identify common-mode risks.

---

## 17. Portfolio Learning

Portfolio governance should identify recurring patterns such as:

- repeated model failures;
- repeated boundary events;
- common dependency failures;
- recurring human-control problems;
- repeated security findings;
- recurring evidence gaps;
- repeated revalidation triggers.

These patterns may indicate systemic governance weaknesses.

---

## 18. Knowledge Repository

The organisation should maintain a controlled **Defence AI Lessons and Knowledge Repository**.

The repository may contain:

- validated lessons;
- findings;
- corrective actions;
- assurance observations;
- approved interpretations;
- reusable controls;
- test scenarios;
- environmental lessons;
- human-factor lessons;
- training material;
- governance decisions.

Sensitive or classified information should be handled according to applicable requirements and should not be incorporated into a generic public framework repository.

---

## 19. Knowledge Validation

Not every observation should become an institutional lesson.

Validation should consider:

- evidence quality;
- reliability;
- reproducibility;
- relevance;
- context;
- alternative explanations;
- operational significance.

Lessons should be marked as:

- Proposed;
- Under Review;
- Validated;
- Incorporated;
- Superseded;
- Rejected.

---

## 20. Knowledge Generalisation

Before applying a lesson broadly, the organisation should determine:

- what conditions produced it;
- whether those conditions exist elsewhere;
- whether the lesson applies to other systems;
- whether system architecture differs;
- whether autonomy differs;
- whether environment differs;
- whether human-control arrangements differ.

A lesson should not be generalised beyond its evidence.

---

## 21. Action Management

Validated lessons should produce defined actions where appropriate.

Actions may include:

- control modification;
- policy update;
- requirement change;
- additional TEVV;
- environmental testing;
- training;
- monitoring enhancement;
- configuration change;
- security improvement;
- authorisation condition;
- restriction;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

Each action should have an owner and completion criteria.

---

## 22. Verification

Actions arising from lessons should be verified.

Verification should establish whether:

- the intended change was implemented;
- the underlying problem was addressed;
- unintended effects were introduced;
- evidence supports closure;
- additional assurance is required.

A lesson should not be considered closed merely because an action was assigned.

---

## 23. Impact on Operational Authorisation

A validated lesson may affect:

- authorisation conditions;
- operational boundaries;
- autonomy;
- human-control requirements;
- environmental limits;
- monitoring;
- configuration;
- risk acceptance;
- validity period.

Material impacts should trigger the applicable change, revalidation or reauthorisation process.

---

## 24. Impact on TEVV

Lessons should feed back into TEVV by identifying:

- new test cases;
- previously untested conditions;
- failed assumptions;
- boundary conditions;
- adversarial scenarios;
- human-factor scenarios;
- autonomy transitions;
- environmental conditions.

This prevents repeated failures from being rediscovered operationally.

---

## 25. Impact on Requirements

Where a lesson indicates that an original requirement was incomplete or inappropriate, the organisation should assess whether to:

- revise the requirement;
- introduce a new requirement;
- introduce an acceptance criterion;
- add a control;
- expand the test envelope;
- change the authorisation basis.

---

## 26. Impact on Training and Competence

Lessons may identify requirements for:

- operator training;
- commander education;
- OAIA training;
- technical training;
- assurance training;
- security awareness;
- emergency exercises;
- autonomy-management training.

Training should reflect validated operational knowledge.

---

## 27. Knowledge and Uncertainty

Institutional knowledge should preserve uncertainty.

Where evidence remains inconclusive, the repository should record:

- what is known;
- what is suspected;
- what is unknown;
- what evidence is missing;
- what assumptions remain;
- what action is required.

Unknown should not be converted into assumed safe.

---

## 28. Knowledge Expiry

Lessons can become outdated.

The organisation should review whether lessons remain valid following:

- model changes;
- architecture changes;
- new environments;
- new missions;
- new threats;
- new evidence;
- changed human-control arrangements;
- changed dependencies.

Outdated lessons should be marked as superseded rather than silently retained.

---

## 29. Knowledge Dissemination

Validated lessons should reach relevant:

- authorising authorities;
- commanders;
- OAIA;
- system owners;
- developers;
- assurance personnel;
- security authorities;
- operators;
- acquisition teams;
- training organisations.

Dissemination should respect applicable information-handling requirements.

---

## 30. Lessons and Governance Metrics

Lesson management may be measured through:

- lessons captured;
- validated lessons;
- time to validation;
- corrective actions;
- overdue actions;
- repeated lessons;
- recurring failure modes;
- lessons incorporated into TEVV;
- lessons incorporated into authorisation conditions;
- lessons resulting in revalidation or reauthorisation.

Metrics should assess learning effectiveness rather than reward high reporting volume.

---

## 31. Repeated Failure

Repeated occurrence of a substantially similar issue should trigger additional governance scrutiny.

Possible responses include:

**Investigate → Expand Assurance → Restrict → Revalidate → Reauthorise → Suspend**

Repeated failure may indicate a systemic weakness rather than isolated operator error.

---

## 32. Knowledge Handover

When responsibility changes, relevant validated knowledge should be transferred.

Handover should include:

- active lessons;
- unresolved findings;
- open actions;
- known limitations;
- recent incidents;
- assurance concerns;
- environmental observations;
- configuration implications.

This supports continuity of governance.

---

## 33. Independent Challenge

Material lessons should be independently challenged where appropriate.

Challenge should consider:

- whether the lesson is supported by evidence;
- whether the root cause is sufficiently understood;
- whether the lesson has been over-generalised;
- whether proposed corrective action is adequate;
- whether broader portfolio implications exist.

---

## 34. Governance Review

The organisation should periodically review:

- quality of lessons;
- recurring findings;
- effectiveness of corrective actions;
- systemic patterns;
- knowledge gaps;
- dissemination;
- incorporation into TEVV;
- impact on authorisation;
- emerging risks.

---

## 35. Golden Thread

Lessons should connect back into the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Incident/Change → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation**

This creates a feedback loop rather than a one-way governance process.

---

## 36. Governance Questions

The organisation should be able to answer:

1. What have we learned from operational employment?
2. Which lessons are supported by reliable evidence?
3. Which findings remain unresolved?
4. Are near misses being captured?
5. Are unexpected AI behaviours being investigated?
6. Are lessons being generalised appropriately?
7. Are recurring problems visible across the portfolio?
8. Have lessons changed TEVV?
9. Have lessons changed authorisation conditions?
10. Have lessons changed training?
11. Are corrective actions actually effective?
12. Which lessons have become obsolete?
13. Who is responsible for maintaining institutional knowledge?

---

## 37. Core Rule

> **D-AIGAAF shall treat operational experience, incidents, near misses, unexpected AI behaviour, assurance findings and emerging evidence as inputs to continuous governance learning. Material lessons shall be captured, evidence-assessed, validated, appropriately generalised and translated into corrective action, assurance improvements, TEVV, training, requirements or authorisation changes where necessary. Repeated failures and systemic patterns shall receive enhanced scrutiny. Knowledge shall preserve uncertainty, remain traceable to evidence and be reviewed for continued relevance so that operational experience continuously strengthens Defence AI governance rather than remaining isolated within individual capabilities or organisations.**
