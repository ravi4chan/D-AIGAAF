# 49 — Authorisation Training, Exercises and Competence

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the training, exercising and competence requirements needed to ensure that personnel can effectively implement D-AIGAAF operational authorisation.

A governance framework is only effective when the people responsible for authorising, operating, assuring, securing and managing Defence AI capabilities understand their roles and can perform them under realistic conditions.

---

## 2. Core Principle

> **Personnel exercising Defence AI governance and operational authority shall possess competence proportionate to their responsibilities, and that competence shall be demonstrated and maintained through appropriate training, exercises and evaluation.**

Appointment to a role should not by itself be treated as evidence of competence.

---

## 3. Competence Model

D-AIGAAF competence should combine:

- role knowledge;
- operational understanding;
- AI literacy;
- risk understanding;
- human-control awareness;
- assurance literacy;
- security awareness;
- decision-making ability;
- communication;
- emergency response.

The required depth should increase with consequence and autonomy.

---

## 4. Role-Based Competence

Training should be tailored to the responsibilities of:

- Strategic Governance;
- Authorising Authority;
- Command/Operational Authority;
- Risk Owner;
- OAIA;
- Technical Authority;
- Assurance/TEVV Authority;
- Security Authority;
- System Manager;
- Operator/User;
- Maintainer;
- Acquisition personnel;
- Audit and review personnel.

No single role should be assumed to possess all required expertise.

---

## 5. Authorising Authority Competence

Authorising personnel should understand:

- operational authorisation;
- risk acceptance;
- evidence sufficiency;
- uncertainty;
- autonomy;
- human control;
- operational boundaries;
- environmental limitations;
- security;
- dependencies;
- conditions and restrictions;
- suspension and revocation;
- revalidation and reauthorisation.

The Authorising Authority should be able to challenge technical and assurance advice without needing to reproduce specialist analysis.

---

## 6. Command and Operational Authority Competence

Command personnel should understand:

- what AI is authorised to do;
- what it is not authorised to do;
- limitations;
- uncertainty;
- autonomy level;
- human decision rights;
- operational boundaries;
- escalation;
- intervention;
- consequences of continued operation outside the authorisation basis.

The objective is informed command judgement, not technical model development.

---

## 7. Operational AI Advisor Competence

The OAIA should bridge:

**Operational Need ↔ AI Capability ↔ Risk ↔ Assurance ↔ Human Authority**

Competence should include:

- operational context;
- AI fundamentals;
- AI limitations;
- uncertainty;
- model/system behaviour;
- risk;
- autonomy;
- TEVV;
- human factors;
- security;
- operational environment;
- authorisation governance;
- communication with command and technical personnel.

The OAIA provides informed advice but does not automatically hold operational authority.

---

## 8. Technical Authority Competence

Technical personnel should understand:

- system architecture;
- configuration;
- model lifecycle;
- performance;
- reliability;
- security;
- dependencies;
- technical limitations;
- change control;
- rollback;
- monitoring;
- failure modes.

Technical competence should include understanding when a technical change may have operational-authorisation implications.

---

## 9. Assurance and TEVV Competence

Assurance personnel should be capable of assessing:

- evidence quality;
- test design;
- reliability;
- robustness;
- adversarial resilience;
- operational environment;
- human-AI interaction;
- security;
- autonomy;
- mission effectiveness;
- uncertainty;
- limitations.

They should be able to distinguish demonstrated capability from unsupported claims.

---

## 10. Operator Competence

Operators should understand:

- authorised functions;
- operational boundaries;
- AI outputs;
- uncertainty;
- system limitations;
- autonomy state;
- intervention;
- override;
- fail-safe;
- escalation;
- incident reporting;
- configuration awareness.

Operators should know when not to rely on an AI output.

---

## 11. AI Literacy

AI literacy should cover, at an appropriate level:

- models;
- data;
- training;
- inference;
- uncertainty;
- errors;
- bias;
- distribution shift;
- hallucination/confabulation;
- adversarial manipulation;
- model updates;
- configuration;
- human-AI interaction.

Training should emphasise that fluent output does not establish correctness.

---

## 12. Risk Literacy

Personnel should understand:

- consequence;
- likelihood;
- uncertainty;
- residual risk;
- risk controls;
- risk acceptance;
- escalation thresholds;
- changing risk;
- cumulative and systemic risk.

Risk should be considered in operational context rather than as an isolated numerical value.

---

## 13. Autonomy Literacy

Personnel should understand the difference between:

- assistance;
- recommendation;
- human-authorised action;
- supervised autonomy;
- independent consequential autonomy.

The D-AIGAAF working scale is:

**A0 → A1 → A2 → A3 → A4 → A5**

Personnel should understand that increasing autonomy generally increases the importance of assurance, human control and operational boundaries.

---

## 14. Human-Control Competence

Personnel should be trained to recognise:

- automation bias;
- over-reliance;
- loss of situational awareness;
- excessive workload;
- unclear responsibility;
- failed intervention;
- inappropriate delegation;
- inappropriate trust in AI outputs.

Training should reinforce that human authority is meaningful only when the human can realistically exercise it.

---

## 15. Uncertainty and AI Output Interpretation

Personnel should be able to distinguish:

- fact;
- inference;
- recommendation;
- prediction;
- uncertainty;
- missing information.

They should understand that AI confidence indicators, where provided, do not necessarily equal real-world correctness.

---

## 16. Boundary Awareness

Personnel should know:

- mission boundaries;
- environmental boundaries;
- autonomy boundaries;
- human-authority boundaries;
- configuration boundaries;
- data boundaries;
- dependency boundaries;
- prohibited uses.

Technical ability must not be interpreted as operational permission.

---

## 17. Security Competence

Relevant personnel should understand:

- model integrity;
- data integrity;
- adversarial inputs;
- unauthorised access;
- configuration compromise;
- supply-chain risks;
- external dependencies;
- security reporting;
- containment;
- recovery.

Security competence should reflect the consequence of the capability.

---

## 18. Emergency Competence

Personnel should be trained for:

- unexpected AI behaviour;
- loss of communications;
- sensor degradation;
- navigation uncertainty;
- loss of human control;
- security incidents;
- dependency failure;
- fail-safe activation;
- environmental changes.

Exercises should establish whether personnel can move the capability toward:

**Restricted → Reduced Autonomy → Human Control → Safe State → Suspended**

when required.

---

## 19. Exercise Programme

The organisation should maintain an exercise programme covering:

- normal authorisation;
- operational readiness;
- deployment;
- employment;
- degraded operation;
- disconnected operation;
- emergency response;
- loss of human control;
- autonomy transitions;
- security incidents;
- boundary violations;
- system failure;
- authority transfer;
- suspension and restoration.

---

## 20. Tabletop Exercises

Tabletop exercises should test governance decisions without necessarily operating the actual system.

Scenarios may examine:

- conflicting authorities;
- uncertain AI output;
- material model change;
- unexpected behaviour;
- environmental change;
- security compromise;
- loss of communication;
- high-consequence decision;
- suspension trigger.

The objective is decision quality and coordination.

---

## 21. Simulation

Where practicable, simulation should test:

- human-AI interaction;
- workload;
- uncertainty;
- autonomy transitions;
- intervention;
- environmental changes;
- degraded communications;
- system failures;
- competing information.

Simulation should be used to supplement, not automatically replace, relevant operational testing.

---

## 22. Operational Exercises

Operational exercises should evaluate whether governance works under realistic conditions.

They should examine:

- decision-making;
- authority;
- human control;
- monitoring;
- escalation;
- fail-safe;
- communication;
- environmental conditions;
- system behaviour.

Exercises should generate evidence and lessons.

---

## 23. Red-Team and Adversarial Exercises

Where appropriate, exercises should challenge:

- model behaviour;
- system boundaries;
- human assumptions;
- interfaces;
- security controls;
- autonomy controls;
- data integrity;
- monitoring.

The purpose is to identify weaknesses before they become operational incidents.

---

## 24. Authority and Decision Exercises

Exercises should test whether personnel know:

- who can authorise;
- who can restrict;
- who can suspend;
- who can revoke;
- who accepts risk;
- who can change autonomy;
- who can approve emergency action;
- when escalation is required.

Ambiguity discovered during an exercise should be treated as a governance finding.

---

## 25. Fail-Safe Exercises

Fail-safe exercises should establish whether:

- intervention works;
- autonomy can be reduced;
- safe state can be reached;
- suspension works;
- recovery is controlled;
- personnel understand the procedure.

Fail-safe exercises should be conducted under conditions appropriate to the capability's risk.

---

## 26. Degraded and Disconnected Exercises

Exercises should test governance when:

- communication is degraded;
- communication is unavailable;
- external services fail;
- data is incomplete;
- sensors degrade;
- personnel are unavailable;
- normal command processes are disrupted.

The exercise should verify that degraded conditions do not create implied AI authority.

---

## 27. Competence Assessment

Competence may be assessed through:

- knowledge assessment;
- practical demonstration;
- simulation;
- tabletop exercise;
- operational exercise;
- observation;
- scenario-based decision assessment.

The assessment method should match the role and consequence.

---

## 28. Competence Levels

A working competence model may use:

### K1 — Awareness

Understands fundamental concepts and responsibilities.

### K2 — Practitioner

Can perform assigned governance or operational tasks.

### K3 — Advanced Practitioner

Can handle complex or degraded scenarios.

### K4 — Authority/Specialist

Can make or advise on high-consequence decisions within defined authority.

These levels should be adapted to organisational requirements.

---

## 29. Competence Currency

Competence should be reviewed when:

- role changes;
- system changes;
- autonomy increases;
- mission changes;
- new threats emerge;
- procedures change;
- significant incidents occur;
- extended absence occurs.

High-consequence roles should have defined currency requirements.

---

## 30. Training After Incidents

Material incidents should trigger assessment of whether:

- training contributed to the event;
- procedures were understood;
- authority was clear;
- personnel recognised the warning signs;
- emergency actions were effective.

Training should be updated where evidence indicates a gap.

---

## 31. Lessons Integration

Training and exercises should feed the D-AIGAAF learning cycle:

**Exercise → Observation → Finding → Lesson → Corrective Action → Verification → Training Update**

Validated lessons should be incorporated into future exercises where relevant.

---

## 32. Multi-Role and Cross-Functional Exercises

Exercises should sometimes involve:

- command;
- OAIA;
- operators;
- technical teams;
- assurance;
- security;
- risk;
- legal/policy;
- acquisition.

This tests whether governance works across organisational boundaries rather than only within individual roles.

---

## 33. Authority Transfer Exercises

Exercises should test:

- shift handover;
- commander succession;
- delegation;
- emergency transfer;
- organisational transfer;
- degraded communication.

The receiving authority should demonstrate understanding of current:

- authorisation;
- restrictions;
- autonomy;
- configuration;
- risks;
- incidents;
- readiness.

---

## 34. Training Records

A controlled competence record should identify:

- individual or role;
- required competence;
- completed training;
- assessment;
- exercise participation;
- competence level;
- validity;
- gaps;
- remediation;
- next review.

Access and retention should follow applicable requirements.

---

## 35. Exercise Records

Exercise records should capture:

- scenario;
- objective;
- participants;
- system/configuration;
- environment;
- decisions;
- observations;
- findings;
- incidents;
- lessons;
- corrective actions;
- evidence;
- follow-up.

Exercise results should not be treated as evidence of capability beyond what was actually demonstrated.

---

## 36. Competence and Authorisation

For high-consequence capabilities, required personnel competence should be an explicit authorisation condition where appropriate.

Failure to maintain required competence may result in:

- additional supervision;
- restriction;
- reduced autonomy;
- temporary withdrawal of authority;
- reassignment;
- additional training.

---

## 37. Organisational Resilience

The organisation should avoid single-person dependency for critical governance functions.

Where practicable, there should be:

- qualified alternates;
- succession arrangements;
- cross-training;
- documented procedures;
- authority-transfer mechanisms.

Continuity arrangements should preserve human authority during personnel disruption.

---

## 38. Competence Metrics

The organisation may monitor:

- training completion;
- competence assessment;
- exercise completion;
- failed assessments;
- overdue currency;
- recurring competence gaps;
- emergency exercise performance;
- authority ambiguity findings;
- lessons incorporated into training.

Metrics should support improvement rather than encourage superficial completion.

---

## 39. Governance Questions

The organisation should be able to answer:

1. Does every consequential role have defined competence requirements?
2. Can authorising authorities evaluate evidence and residual risk?
3. Can commanders understand AI limitations and uncertainty?
4. Can OAIAs bridge operational and technical perspectives?
5. Can operators recognise when not to trust an AI output?
6. Can personnel manage autonomy transitions?
7. Can personnel respond to loss of human control?
8. Have degraded and disconnected conditions been exercised?
9. Have suspension and restoration been practised?
10. Are authority transfers understood?
11. Are incidents changing training?
12. Is competence maintained when systems, missions or risks change?

---

## 40. Golden Thread

Competence should remain connected to:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Incident/Change → Learning → Training → Revalidation/Reauthorisation**

---

## 41. Core Rule

> **D-AIGAAF shall require personnel exercising Defence AI governance, operational authority, technical responsibility, assurance, security and system operation to possess and maintain competence proportionate to their role, consequence and autonomy. Competence shall be demonstrated through appropriate training, assessment and exercises, including realistic degraded, disconnected, adversarial and emergency conditions where relevant. Material competence gaps shall be treated as governance risks and may require additional supervision, restriction, reduced autonomy, reassignment, training or suspension of authority. Training and exercises shall continuously incorporate validated operational lessons so that human competence remains aligned with evolving AI capabilities, risks and operational conditions.**
