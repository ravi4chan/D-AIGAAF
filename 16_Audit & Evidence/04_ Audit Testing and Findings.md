# 04 — Audit Testing and Findings

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how D-AIGAAF audit teams test controls and criteria, evaluate exceptions, formulate findings, and reach evidence-based conclusions.

It establishes a consistent approach for:

- audit testing;
- test execution;
- exception identification;
- finding formulation;
- severity assessment;
- root-cause consideration;
- conclusion development;
- review and challenge.

Detailed corrective action and follow-up are addressed in `06_Nonconformity_Corrective_Action_and_Follow_Up.md`.

---

## 2. Testing Principle

Audit testing should determine whether the audited activity satisfies the defined criteria and whether relevant controls are designed, implemented, operating, and effective.

The basic sequence is:

**Criterion → Control → Test Objective → Test Procedure → Evidence → Result → Finding/Conclusion**

Testing should be sufficient to support the intended conclusion and proportionate to risk and consequence.

---

## 3. Test Objective

Each material test should have a defined objective.

A test objective should state what the auditor intends to determine, such as:

- whether a required control exists;
- whether it is implemented;
- whether it operated during a defined period;
- whether it was effective;
- whether an authorisation condition was followed;
- whether evidence supports a governance claim.

---

## 4. Test Criteria

Testing should be performed against defined criteria.

Criteria may include:

- policy requirements;
- D-AIGAAF requirements;
- control objectives;
- procedures;
- authorisation conditions;
- contractual requirements;
- technical requirements;
- legal or regulatory obligations.

The applicable criterion should be identifiable for each material test.

---

## 5. Test Methods

Appropriate methods may include:

- inspection;
- inquiry;
- observation;
- walkthrough;
- sampling;
- reperformance;
- configuration examination;
- record review;
- independent corroboration;
- controlled technical testing.

The selected method should be appropriate to the control and audit objective.

---

## 6. Inspection

Inspection examines records, configurations, approvals, outputs, or other evidence.

Inspection may establish:

- existence;
- completeness;
- configuration;
- approval;
- implementation;
- historical operation.

Inspection alone may not establish effectiveness.

---

## 7. Inquiry

Inquiry involves obtaining information from relevant personnel.

Inquiry can provide context and identify evidence sources.

Material conclusions should not rely solely on inquiry where appropriate independent evidence can be obtained.

---

## 8. Observation

Observation involves directly observing a process, control, system, or activity.

Observation can provide strong evidence of actual operation but represents the observed conditions at a particular time.

Auditors should consider whether observed behaviour is representative.

---

## 9. Walkthrough

A walkthrough follows a process from initiation through completion.

It may be used to determine whether:

- roles are understood;
- controls are connected;
- records are generated;
- decisions follow defined authority;
- exceptions are handled.

Walkthroughs are particularly useful for identifying control gaps before detailed testing.

---

## 10. Reperformance

Reperformance involves independently repeating or verifying a control activity.

It can provide stronger evidence where practical, particularly for:

- calculations;
- configuration checks;
- approval workflows;
- data transformations;
- control logic.

---

## 11. Technical Testing

Technical testing may be used where audit conclusions depend on system behaviour.

It may examine:

- configuration;
- access control;
- logging;
- version control;
- security controls;
- autonomy restrictions;
- monitoring;
- fail-safe mechanisms.

Audit technical testing should have a defined governance purpose and should not duplicate TEVV without justification.

---

## 12. Sampling

Sampling may be used where testing the complete population is impractical.

Sampling should consider:

- population;
- risk;
- consequence;
- variability;
- frequency;
- previous findings;
- recent changes;
- unusual cases.

Sampling limitations should be reflected in conclusions.

---

## 13. Test Selection

Test selection should be risk-informed.

Greater attention may be appropriate for:

- high-consequence decisions;
- higher autonomy;
- critical controls;
- recent changes;
- known weaknesses;
- unusual events;
- incidents;
- boundary conditions.

Random selection alone may not adequately address high-risk cases.

---

## 14. Test Population

Where sampling or recurring-control testing is used, the population should be defined.

The population should specify:

- relevant activity;
- period;
- system;
- configuration;
- environment;
- inclusion criteria.

An incomplete population can produce misleading test results.

---

## 15. Test Procedure

Material tests should document:

- objective;
- criterion;
- population or subject;
- method;
- evidence;
- procedure;
- expected result;
- actual result;
- exception;
- conclusion.

The level of documentation should be proportionate to significance.

---

## 16. Expected Result

Where appropriate, each test should define the expected condition before reviewing the result.

This reduces the risk of post-hoc interpretation.

Expected results should be derived from the applicable criterion or control objective.

---

## 17. Actual Result

The actual result should describe what was observed or established through evidence.

It should distinguish:

- verified fact;
- management explanation;
- auditor interpretation;
- unresolved uncertainty.

---

## 18. Test Exceptions

An exception occurs where the observed condition differs from the expected condition.

Examples include:

- missing approval;
- incorrect configuration;
- incomplete evidence;
- control not operating;
- authorisation condition not followed;
- failed monitoring threshold;
- human-control mechanism unavailable.

Exceptions should be assessed rather than automatically classified as findings.

---

## 19. Exception Evaluation

Each material exception should be assessed for:

- consequence;
- likelihood;
- frequency;
- duration;
- scope;
- recurrence;
- control significance;
- autonomy implications;
- human-control implications;
- security implications;
- systemic significance.

---

## 20. Finding Definition

A finding is a substantiated condition identified through audit that requires attention.

A finding may represent:

- nonconformity;
- control weakness;
- evidence deficiency;
- significant risk;
- authorisation breach;
- systemic governance weakness.

Findings should be supported by sufficient evidence.

---

## 21. Finding Structure

A material finding should normally contain:

**Condition → Criterion → Evidence → Cause/Contributing Factors → Effect/Impact → Risk → Requirement for Action**

This structure allows the finding to be understood and remediated.

---

## 22. Condition

The condition describes what the audit established.

It should be:

- factual;
- specific;
- evidence-based;
- within scope;
- free from unnecessary speculation.

---

## 23. Criterion

The criterion identifies what should have occurred.

It may be derived from:

- policy;
- D-AIGAAF;
- procedure;
- authorisation;
- law;
- regulation;
- contract;
- approved control objective.

The criterion should be clearly stated.

---

## 24. Evidence

The finding should identify sufficient evidence supporting the condition.

Evidence should be traceable to the audit evidence record.

Where evidence is incomplete, the finding should state the limitation.

---

## 25. Cause

Where practical, the audit should consider why the condition occurred.

Possible causes include:

- inadequate design;
- unclear responsibility;
- insufficient competence;
- process failure;
- technical failure;
- configuration error;
- dependency failure;
- inadequate monitoring;
- ineffective change management.

Cause analysis should avoid unsupported speculation.

---

## 26. Contributing Factors

More than one factor may contribute to a finding.

These may include:

- workload;
- environmental conditions;
- human factors;
- supplier dependency;
- operational pressure;
- unclear authority;
- inadequate information;
- organisational incentives.

Contributing factors should be distinguished from established root cause.

---

## 27. Effect and Impact

The finding should consider actual or potential effect.

Impact may involve:

- safety;
- human control;
- autonomy;
- mission performance;
- security;
- legal compliance;
- operational authorisation;
- data integrity;
- accountability;
- continuity.

Potential impact should be distinguished from demonstrated impact.

---

## 28. Finding Severity

Finding severity should consider:

- consequence;
- likelihood;
- exposure;
- recurrence;
- duration;
- control failure;
- autonomy;
- human-control implications;
- security;
- authorisation;
- systemic effect.

A suggested hierarchy is:

- **Observation**
- **Minor**
- **Significant**
- **Critical**

Organisations may adapt terminology while preserving the underlying principles.

---

## 29. Observation

An observation identifies a condition that may warrant attention but does not currently demonstrate a material control or requirement failure.

Observations should not be used to conceal genuine nonconformities.

---

## 30. Minor Finding

A minor finding represents a limited weakness that does not materially undermine the relevant governance objective but requires correction or improvement.

Examples may include:

- isolated process weakness;
- limited documentation deficiency;
- localised control inconsistency.

---

## 31. Significant Finding

A significant finding represents a material control weakness, requirement failure, or risk that could materially affect governance effectiveness.

Examples may include:

- recurring control failure;
- material evidence gap;
- failure of an important authorisation condition;
- significant human-control weakness;
- major configuration governance deficiency.

---

## 32. Critical Finding

A critical finding indicates a condition that may create immediate or severe consequences for:

- human safety;
- human control;
- authorised autonomy;
- mission-critical operation;
- security;
- legal compliance;
- operational authorisation.

Critical findings should be escalated promptly.

---

## 33. Autonomy-Related Findings

Particular attention should be given to findings involving:

- unauthorised autonomy;
- autonomy beyond approved boundaries;
- failure of autonomy transition controls;
- unavailable human intervention;
- ineffective override;
- inadequate safe-state behaviour;
- inability to determine current autonomy state.

Where observed autonomy exceeds authorised autonomy, the issue should be treated as potentially critical until appropriately assessed.

---

## 34. Human-Control Findings

Findings may arise where:

- responsible authority is unclear;
- personnel lack required competence;
- relevant information is unavailable;
- uncertainty is inadequately communicated;
- intervention is impractical;
- override is ineffective;
- workload prevents meaningful supervision;
- decisions cannot be reconstructed.

Human presence alone does not establish human control.

---

## 35. Evidence Findings

A finding may concern evidence rather than the underlying control.

Examples include:

- missing records;
- unreliable logs;
- stale evidence;
- inadequate provenance;
- contradictory records;
- configuration mismatch;
- inability to reconstruct a consequential decision.

Evidence weaknesses can materially reduce assurance even where the underlying control may have operated.

---

## 36. Systemic Findings

Repeated or related findings should be analysed for systemic causes.

Indicators include:

- similar failures across capabilities;
- recurring evidence deficiencies;
- repeated authorisation breaches;
- widespread configuration drift;
- recurring human-control weaknesses;
- common supplier failures.

Systemic issues should be escalated beyond individual process owners where appropriate.

---

## 37. Finding Validation

Before finalisation, material findings should be reviewed for:

- criterion accuracy;
- evidence sufficiency;
- factual correctness;
- scope;
- severity;
- causal reasoning;
- impact;
- consistency.

Audited parties should normally have an opportunity to provide factual clarification.

---

## 38. Disagreement

Where the audited party disagrees with a finding:

- the disagreement should be documented;
- relevant evidence should be reviewed;
- factual errors should be corrected;
- substantive differences should be escalated through the audit governance process;
- the auditor should retain responsibility for the independent conclusion.

Disagreement should not automatically invalidate a finding.

---

## 39. Finding Closure

A finding should not be considered closed merely because a corrective action was reported as completed.

Closure should require appropriate verification that:

- the required action occurred;
- the underlying condition was addressed;
- relevant risk was reduced;
- the control is effective where applicable.

Detailed follow-up is addressed in `06_Nonconformity_Corrective_Action_and_Follow_Up.md`.

---

## 40. Audit Conclusion

The audit conclusion should reflect the combined results of testing.

Possible conclusions include:

- criteria substantially satisfied;
- criteria satisfied with minor issues;
- partial conformity;
- material control weakness;
- significant nonconformity;
- insufficient evidence to conclude.

The conclusion should not exceed the evidence and scope.

---

## 41. Evidence of Testing

Material testing should leave sufficient evidence to demonstrate:

- what was tested;
- against what criterion;
- how it was tested;
- what evidence was considered;
- what result was obtained;
- what conclusion followed.

This supports audit reproducibility and review.

---

## 42. Test Documentation Quality

Test records should be:

- clear;
- concise;
- traceable;
- factual;
- reproducible where practical;
- appropriately protected.

Documentation should distinguish working notes from final evidence and conclusions.

---

## 43. Quality Review

Material test results and findings should undergo appropriate review.

Review should consider:

- methodology;
- evidence;
- criteria;
- reasoning;
- severity;
- consistency;
- conclusion.

Critical findings should receive heightened review and escalation.

---

## 44. Relationship to TEVV

Audit testing may examine whether required TEVV processes and evidence are governed effectively.

It should not unnecessarily duplicate technical validation or operational trials.

Where TEVV evidence is relied upon, audit should assess its relevance, integrity, scope, currency, and applicability.

---

## 45. Relationship to Incidents

Incident evidence may be used during audit testing.

Where an incident indicates a potential control failure, auditors should consider whether:

- the control failed;
- the control was bypassed;
- the control was insufficient;
- the incident resulted from another cause;
- broader systemic weaknesses exist.

Module 14 governs incident response and investigation.

---

## 46. Relationship to Change

Testing should consider whether recent changes affected the control being audited.

Relevant changes may include:

- model;
- software;
- data;
- hardware;
- configuration;
- interface;
- autonomy;
- environment;
- supplier;
- dependency.

Material change-related findings may trigger revalidation or reauthorisation review.

---

## 47. Relationship to Operational Authorisation

Where audit testing identifies a failure to satisfy an operational authorisation condition, the finding should be assessed for its effect on current authority.

Possible consequences include:

- corrective action;
- enhanced monitoring;
- restriction;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation review;
- suspension.

The authorising authority remains responsible for the authority decision.

---

## 48. Core Testing Model

D-AIGAAF uses:

**Define → Test → Observe → Compare → Evaluate → Find → Classify → Conclude → Verify**

Testing should remain evidence-based, risk-informed, independent, and proportionate.

---

## 49. Core Rule

**An audit finding must be more than an auditor's opinion. It should establish a defined condition against an applicable criterion, supported by sufficient evidence, with proportionate assessment of impact and severity. Testing should establish what actually occurred, while conclusions should remain within the limits of the evidence, methodology, and approved scope.**

---

## 50. Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Audit Planning → Criteria → Testing → Findings → Corrective Action → Verification**
