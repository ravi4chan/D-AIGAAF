# 05 — Audit Independence and Challenge

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes requirements for independence, objectivity, professional judgement, and constructive challenge within D-AIGAAF audit activities.

Its purpose is to ensure that audit conclusions are sufficiently free from inappropriate influence and that material governance claims receive credible independent examination.

---

## 2. Independence Principle

Audit independence means that auditors are sufficiently free from circumstances that could compromise objective judgement.

Independence is not absolute organisational separation in every circumstance.

It is the practical ability to:

- obtain relevant evidence;
- assess it objectively;
- challenge assertions;
- report findings;
- escalate material concerns;
- reach conclusions without inappropriate interference.

---

## 3. Objectivity

Objectivity requires auditors to form conclusions from defined criteria and evidence rather than:

- organisational rank;
- personal preference;
- operational pressure;
- desired outcomes;
- commercial interests;
- prior commitments;
- assumptions;
- confirmation bias.

Professional judgement should remain evidence-based.

---

## 4. Independence from Audited Activity

Where practicable, auditors should not be directly responsible for the design, operation, approval, or ownership of the controls they audit.

Auditors should not audit their own material work without appropriate safeguards.

---

## 5. Conflict of Interest

Potential conflicts may arise from:

- previous responsibility for the audited system;
- personal financial interests;
- supplier relationships;
- reporting relationships;
- close personal relationships;
- participation in design or approval;
- organisational incentives;
- performance objectives.

Material conflicts should be disclosed and appropriately managed.

---

## 6. Self-Review Risk

A self-review risk occurs where an auditor is required to assess work in which they previously had substantive responsibility.

Examples include auditing:

- a control the auditor designed;
- a system the auditor approved;
- a risk assessment the auditor authored;
- a corrective action the auditor implemented.

Where self-review cannot be avoided, independent review should be applied.

---

## 7. Audit Reporting Line

The audit function should have an appropriate reporting route that permits:

- access to governance leadership;
- escalation of unresolved findings;
- protection from inappropriate operational pressure;
- communication of critical concerns.

The precise organisational structure may vary by institution.

---

## 8. Authority to Challenge

Auditors should have sufficient authority to:

- request relevant evidence;
- question assumptions;
- challenge conclusions;
- identify control weaknesses;
- escalate material risks;
- document disagreements.

Audit authority should be defined before substantive audit work begins.

---

## 9. Evidence-Based Challenge

Challenge should be grounded in:

- applicable criteria;
- evidence;
- risk;
- control effectiveness;
- operational context;
- uncertainty.

Challenge should not become disagreement for its own sake.

The objective is to improve the reliability of governance conclusions.

---

## 10. Constructive Challenge

Effective audit challenge should:

1. identify the claim;
2. identify the supporting evidence;
3. identify the applicable criterion;
4. test whether the evidence supports the claim;
5. identify gaps or contradictions;
6. assess impact;
7. determine whether further evidence is required;
8. document the conclusion.

---

## 11. Independence and Technical Competence

Independence does not mean that auditors can operate without subject-matter expertise.

AI-related audits may require knowledge of:

- AI and machine learning;
- data;
- cybersecurity;
- software and systems;
- autonomy;
- human factors;
- safety;
- operational environments;
- AI governance;
- applicable law and policy.

Specialists may support audits while the audit team retains responsibility for the audit conclusion.

---

## 12. Use of Specialists

Specialists may be used for:

- technical assessment;
- cybersecurity;
- AI assurance;
- data analysis;
- safety;
- human factors;
- legal or regulatory interpretation;
- operational context;
- supply-chain assessment.

The role, competence, independence, and contribution of specialists should be documented where material.

---

## 13. Specialist Independence

Specialists should be assessed for conflicts relevant to the area they evaluate.

A technical expert who designed a system may provide valuable knowledge but may require independent review when evaluating that same design.

---

## 14. Management Assertions

Auditors should distinguish management assertions from independently substantiated evidence.

Management explanations can:

- provide context;
- identify evidence;
- explain anomalies;
- clarify processes.

They should not automatically determine the audit conclusion.

---

## 15. Organisational Pressure

Audit teams may encounter pressure to:

- reduce scope;
- delay findings;
- lower severity;
- omit inconvenient evidence;
- avoid escalation;
- accelerate closure;
- align conclusions with expected outcomes.

Such pressure should not compromise audit integrity.

Material pressure should be documented and escalated through appropriate channels.

---

## 16. Commercial Pressure

Where suppliers or commercial interests are involved, audit independence should be protected from:

- contractual pressure;
- revenue considerations;
- procurement relationships;
- supplier influence;
- programme delivery incentives.

Commercial importance should not determine audit conclusions.

---

## 17. Operational Pressure

Operational urgency may justify accelerated audit activity but should not automatically reduce applicable governance requirements.

Where urgent circumstances require abbreviated procedures, the limitation and rationale should be recorded.

---

## 18. Independence Safeguards

Safeguards may include:

- independent reviewer;
- separate reporting line;
- rotation of auditors;
- conflict declarations;
- peer review;
- specialist review;
- governance escalation;
- separation of audit and remediation ownership.

Safeguards should be proportionate to risk.

---

## 19. Auditor Rotation

Periodic rotation may reduce familiarity and self-review risks.

Rotation should not be applied mechanically where continuity and specialist competence are more important.

The objective is to maintain independence without unnecessarily degrading audit quality.

---

## 20. Independence Assessment

Before substantive audit work, the audit team should assess:

- conflicts of interest;
- prior involvement;
- reporting relationships;
- financial interests;
- organisational incentives;
- specialist independence;
- ability to challenge.

The assessment should be recorded for material audits.

---

## 21. Independence During the Audit

Independence should be reassessed if circumstances change.

Triggers may include:

- new responsibilities;
- organisational changes;
- discovery of prior involvement;
- supplier relationships;
- changes in audit scope;
- emerging conflicts.

A material change may require reassignment or additional independent review.

---

## 22. Independence of Evidence

Evidence itself may have varying degrees of independence.

Auditors should consider whether evidence originates from:

- the audited process;
- an independent control;
- an external source;
- a system-generated record;
- a management assertion.

Evidence independence should inform confidence in the conclusion.

---

## 23. Independent Corroboration

Material claims should be independently corroborated where appropriate.

Examples include:

- comparing system records with authorisation records;
- comparing supplier claims with independent evidence;
- comparing reported performance with operational records;
- verifying configuration independently.

Corroboration is particularly important where consequences are high.

---

## 24. Challenge of Risk Acceptance

Auditors should examine whether risk acceptance:

- was made by the appropriate authority;
- used current evidence;
- considered residual risk;
- considered alternatives;
- respected applicable constraints;
- remained within delegated authority.

Audit should challenge the governance process, not substitute its own risk acceptance decision.

---

## 25. Challenge of Autonomy

Where autonomy is consequential, auditors should challenge whether:

- authorised autonomy is clearly defined;
- observed autonomy is consistent with authorisation;
- boundaries are enforceable;
- transitions are controlled;
- human authority remains effective;
- intervention is practicable;
- safe-state mechanisms are available.

---

## 26. Challenge of Human Control

Auditors should not equate:

**Human present = Human informed = Human supervising = Human controlling**

These are distinct conditions.

The audit should determine whether meaningful human control is actually supported by evidence.

---

## 27. Challenge of Evidence Sufficiency

Auditors should challenge claims where:

- evidence is incomplete;
- evidence is stale;
- evidence is indirect;
- evidence is contradictory;
- configuration is uncertain;
- environmental applicability is unclear;
- sampling is insufficient;
- evidence depends entirely on assertion.

The appropriate response may be additional testing, a limitation, or a finding.

---

## 28. Challenge of Confidence

High confidence should not be assigned solely because:

- documentation is extensive;
- testing volume is large;
- no incidents have occurred;
- senior personnel approve the system;
- a supplier provides assurance;
- a system has operated successfully in another environment.

Confidence should reflect evidence quality and applicability.

---

## 29. Challenge of Assumptions

Material assumptions should be identified and tested where practical.

Examples include assumptions about:

- data quality;
- human response time;
- communications;
- sensor availability;
- environmental stability;
- supplier continuity;
- model behaviour;
- autonomy boundaries;
- threat conditions.

Unvalidated assumptions should be visible in conclusions.

---

## 30. Challenge of Positive and Negative Evidence

Auditors should consider both:

- evidence supporting governance claims;
- evidence contradicting or weakening those claims.

Negative evidence should not be discounted simply because it is inconvenient.

---

## 31. Challenge of Previous Conclusions

Auditors may need to revisit previous conclusions when:

- new evidence emerges;
- a material incident occurs;
- a significant change occurs;
- assumptions are invalidated;
- repeated findings occur;
- evidence integrity is questioned.

Previous audit conclusions should not be treated as permanently valid.

---

## 32. Escalation of Unresolved Disagreement

Where material disagreement remains unresolved:

1. document the issue;
2. identify the competing positions;
3. identify supporting evidence;
4. assess applicable criteria;
5. obtain appropriate independent review;
6. escalate to the designated governance authority;
7. preserve the disagreement in the audit record where material.

---

## 33. Auditor Protection

Auditors should have appropriate organisational protection against retaliation or inappropriate consequences for good-faith reporting of material findings.

Protection should support:

- factual reporting;
- independent judgement;
- escalation;
- documentation of concerns.

---

## 34. Independence and Confidentiality

Confidentiality should protect sensitive audit information without preventing appropriate escalation.

Confidentiality must not be used to conceal material findings from authorised governance authorities.

---

## 35. Independence and Sensitive Information

D-AIGAAF is a generic and unclassified framework.

Actual audits may involve sensitive operational, technical, security, or personal information.

Access to such information should be controlled while ensuring that auditors can obtain sufficient evidence to perform their role.

---

## 36. Challenge in High-Consequence AI

For high-consequence AI, independent challenge should receive increased emphasis.

Areas may include:

- safety;
- human control;
- autonomy;
- security;
- mission effectiveness;
- operational environment;
- critical dependencies;
- authorisation conditions.

The higher the potential consequence, the stronger the case for credible independent challenge.

---

## 37. Independent Review

Independent review may be required for:

- critical findings;
- high-consequence AI;
- material evidence limitations;
- significant governance disputes;
- suspected loss of human control;
- unauthorised autonomy;
- major security concerns;
- material authorisation breaches.

The reviewer should have appropriate competence and sufficient independence.

---

## 38. Challenge Records

Material challenge should be recorded where it affects:

- evidence interpretation;
- finding severity;
- audit conclusion;
- scope;
- risk;
- authorisation;
- corrective action.

Records should distinguish between resolved and unresolved disagreements.

---

## 39. Audit Quality and Independence

Audit quality should be assessed together with independence.

A highly independent audit with inadequate technical competence may produce weak conclusions.

A technically excellent audit compromised by conflicts of interest may also produce unreliable conclusions.

D-AIGAAF therefore requires both:

**Competence + Independence + Evidence + Challenge**

---

## 40. Independence Review

At the end of a material audit, the audit function should consider whether independence was maintained throughout the engagement.

Material threats and safeguards should be documented.

---

## 41. Integration with D-AIGAAF

Independence and challenge should integrate with:

- Module 01 — Strategy & Governance;
- Module 03 — Risk & Autonomy;
- Module 09 — TEVV;
- Module 11 — Operational Authorisation;
- Module 13 — Continuous Assurance;
- Module 14 — Incident & Fail-Safe;
- Module 15 — Change & Reauthorisation;
- Module 16 — Audit & Evidence.

Independent challenge should strengthen, not replace, accountable decision-making.

---

## 42. Independence and Governance Assurance

Audit independence supports confidence that governance conclusions are not merely self-attested.

The organisation should be able to distinguish:

**Management Assertion → Internal Control Evidence → Independent Audit Evidence → Governance Conclusion**

This distinction is especially important for high-consequence AI.

---

## 43. Core Independence Model

D-AIGAAF uses:

**Declare → Assess → Safeguard → Audit → Challenge → Escalate → Review → Record**

Independence should be actively maintained rather than assumed.

---

## 44. Core Rule

**Audit independence is the ability to reach and communicate an objective conclusion without inappropriate influence. D-AIGAAF requires conflicts, self-review risks, organisational pressures, evidence limitations, and material disagreements to be identified and managed so that high-consequence governance decisions receive credible independent challenge.**

---

## 45. Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Audit Planning → Criteria → Testing → Evidence → Independent Challenge → Findings → Corrective Action → Verification**
