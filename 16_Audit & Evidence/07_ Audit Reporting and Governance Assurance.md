# 07 — Audit Reporting and Governance Assurance

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes requirements for reporting audit results and providing governance-level assurance within D-AIGAAF.

The purpose of audit reporting is to communicate, clearly and objectively:

- what was audited;
- why it was audited;
- what criteria were applied;
- what evidence was examined;
- what was tested;
- what was found;
- what conclusions are supported;
- what remains uncertain;
- what corrective action is required;
- what governance decisions may be necessary.

Audit reporting should support accountability without overstating assurance.

---

## 2. Reporting Principle

An audit report should distinguish between:

- observed facts;
- audit evidence;
- findings;
- management responses;
- auditor conclusions;
- unresolved uncertainty;
- recommendations;
- required actions.

A management response should not be presented as an auditor conclusion.

---

## 3. Audit Report

An audit report should normally identify:

- audit identifier;
- audit objective;
- audit scope;
- audit criteria;
- audit period;
- methodology;
- evidence basis;
- limitations;
- findings;
- overall conclusion;
- corrective-action requirements;
- follow-up requirements.

The level of detail should be proportionate to risk and consequence.

---

## 4. Executive Summary

For significant audits, an executive summary should provide a concise view of:

- purpose;
- scope;
- major findings;
- significant risks;
- assurance limitations;
- critical corrective actions;
- governance implications.

The executive summary should not conceal material negative findings.

---

## 5. Audit Objective and Scope

The report should state whether the audit objective was:

- fully addressed;
- partially addressed;
- not addressed.

Any material scope limitations should be disclosed.

Conclusions should remain within the approved audit scope unless the scope was formally expanded.

---

## 6. Audit Criteria

The report should identify the principal criteria against which the audited activity was assessed.

Criteria may include:

- D-AIGAAF requirements;
- organisational policies;
- applicable law;
- standards;
- contractual requirements;
- authorisation conditions;
- approved controls;
- documented procedures.

---

## 7. Evidence Basis

The report should describe the evidence supporting the audit conclusion.

Where appropriate, it should identify:

- evidence categories;
- testing performed;
- sampling;
- significant limitations;
- evidence gaps;
- contradictory evidence.

A conclusion should not imply greater evidence coverage than was actually obtained.

---

## 8. Audit Findings

Findings should be presented clearly and consistently.

A material finding should normally identify:

**Condition → Criterion → Evidence → Cause/Contributing Factors → Effect/Impact → Risk → Requirement for Action**

Where cause cannot be established, the report should not present an assumption as fact.

---

## 9. Finding Severity

D-AIGAAF may use:

- **Observation** — improvement opportunity or condition requiring attention but not demonstrating a material control failure.
- **Minor** — limited nonconformity or weakness with bounded impact.
- **Significant** — material weakness that may affect governance, assurance, compliance, safety, security, human control, autonomy or operational suitability.
- **Critical** — condition presenting potentially unacceptable or immediate consequence requiring urgent governance action.

Severity should be evidence-based.

---

## 10. Critical Findings

Critical findings should be escalated without waiting for routine reporting cycles where necessary.

Possible responses include:

- immediate containment;
- restriction;
- reduction of autonomy;
- enhanced monitoring;
- suspension;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation review.

Audit reporting does not itself grant or withdraw operational authority unless the organisation's governance arrangements explicitly provide such authority.

---

## 11. Overall Audit Conclusion

The auditor should provide a conclusion proportionate to the evidence.

Possible outcomes include:

- effective and conforming;
- generally effective with limitations;
- partially effective;
- materially ineffective;
- insufficient evidence to conclude.

The conclusion should clearly state significant limitations.

---

## 12. Assurance Confidence

Where useful, the report may state confidence in the audit conclusion.

Confidence should consider:

- evidence quality;
- evidence coverage;
- evidence reliability;
- testing depth;
- independence;
- unresolved contradictions;
- sampling limitations;
- system complexity;
- environmental relevance;
- configuration relevance.

High confidence should not be assigned merely because the audit produced a large volume of records.

---

## 13. Evidence Limitations

Material limitations should be explicitly reported.

Examples include:

- unavailable evidence;
- incomplete records;
- restricted access;
- limited sampling;
- configuration uncertainty;
- untested operating conditions;
- dependence on third-party evidence;
- contradictory information.

Limitations may reduce the strength of the audit conclusion.

---

## 14. Management Response

Management should have an opportunity to respond to findings.

Management responses may include:

- acceptance;
- disagreement;
- corrective action;
- risk treatment;
- explanation;
- additional evidence.

Management responses should remain distinguishable from the independent audit conclusion.

---

## 15. Corrective Action Reporting

Where corrective action is required, the report should identify:

- finding;
- required action;
- responsible owner;
- priority;
- target date;
- verification requirement.

Critical or significant actions should receive appropriate governance visibility.

---

## 16. Corrective Action Status

Follow-up reporting may classify actions as:

- Open;
- In progress;
- Evidence submitted;
- Pending verification;
- Verified;
- Partially verified;
- Ineffective;
- Closed;
- Overdue.

Closure should require appropriate verification.

---

## 17. Escalation

Audit results should be escalated when findings may materially affect:

- safety;
- security;
- human control;
- autonomy;
- operational authorisation;
- mission effectiveness;
- legal or regulatory compliance;
- critical dependencies;
- assurance confidence.

Escalation should follow defined decision rights.

---

## 18. Governance-Level Assurance

Audit reporting should enable governance authorities to understand whether governance arrangements are:

- established;
- implemented;
- operating;
- effective;
- appropriately evidenced.

Governance assurance should not be inferred solely from policy existence.

---

## 19. Compliance Versus Effectiveness

Audit reporting should distinguish:

### Compliance
Whether a requirement has been met.

### Control Effectiveness
Whether the control is appropriately designed and operating effectively.

### Assurance
Whether sufficient reliable evidence supports confidence in the relevant claim.

A system may be compliant with a documented requirement while still having ineffective controls.

---

## 20. Autonomy Reporting

Where autonomy is relevant, audit reporting should consider:

- authorised autonomy level;
- implemented autonomy;
- observed behaviour;
- autonomy boundaries;
- transition controls;
- human intervention;
- override capability;
- unexpected autonomy;
- evidence supporting autonomy claims.

Technical capability should not be treated as operational authority.

---

## 21. Human Control Reporting

Where human control is material, reporting should address whether authorised personnel can:

- understand relevant AI outputs;
- recognise uncertainty;
- maintain situational awareness;
- supervise the system;
- intervene;
- override;
- pause;
- terminate or move to a safe state where required.

Human presence alone should not be treated as evidence of meaningful human control.

---

## 22. Security Reporting

Audit reports should identify material weaknesses affecting:

- AI security controls;
- adversarial resilience;
- access control;
- model or data integrity;
- supply-chain security;
- dependencies;
- monitoring;
- incident response.

Security findings should be linked to evidence and risk.

---

## 23. Environment Reporting

Where relevant, reports should state whether controls and evidence were assessed under appropriate operational environments.

Consider:

- nominal conditions;
- degraded conditions;
- disconnected conditions;
- adversarial conditions;
- environmental boundaries;
- human operating conditions.

Evidence from one environment should not automatically be generalised to another.

---

## 24. Change and Configuration Reporting

Where audit findings relate to changes, reporting should identify relevant:

- system version;
- model version;
- data configuration;
- software;
- hardware;
- interfaces;
- dependencies;
- autonomy configuration.

Uncontrolled configuration changes may invalidate previously obtained evidence.

---

## 25. Incident-Related Reporting

Where an audit follows or includes an incident, reporting should distinguish:

- incident facts;
- investigation results;
- control weaknesses;
- root causes;
- corrective actions;
- assurance impact.

Incident records should remain linked to the audit where appropriate.

---

## 26. Reporting to Authorising Authorities

Material audit results should be available to the relevant authorising authority when they affect:

- authorisation conditions;
- operational boundaries;
- autonomy;
- risk acceptance;
- human authority;
- assurance confidence;
- continued operational suitability.

Audit provides evidence and independent assessment; operational authority remains governed by Module 11.

---

## 27. Governance Dashboard

Organisations may maintain governance dashboards showing:

- audit coverage;
- open findings;
- critical findings;
- overdue actions;
- recurring findings;
- systemic issues;
- assurance confidence;
- evidence gaps;
- corrective-action effectiveness;
- high-consequence AI exposure.

Dashboards should not conceal material issues through excessive aggregation.

---

## 28. Audit Coverage

Governance reporting should identify important areas that have not been audited or have only limited assurance.

Coverage gaps may themselves represent governance risk.

Potential gaps include:

- high-consequence use cases;
- autonomy;
- human control;
- operational environments;
- critical suppliers;
- data;
- AI security;
- change management;
- incident response.

---

## 29. Systemic Findings

Where similar findings occur across multiple AI capabilities, they should be assessed for systemic significance.

Possible systemic causes include:

- common policy weakness;
- common control weakness;
- shared supplier dependency;
- inadequate workforce competence;
- weak configuration management;
- insufficient assurance methods.

Systemic findings should be escalated beyond individual system ownership where appropriate.

---

## 30. Reporting Quality Review

Before release, significant audit reports should undergo appropriate quality review.

Review should consider:

- factual accuracy;
- evidence support;
- finding severity;
- conclusion consistency;
- scope compliance;
- independence;
- unresolved disagreement;
- confidentiality;
- required escalation.

---

## 31. Report Approval

The organisation should define who may approve:

- routine audit reports;
- significant findings;
- critical findings;
- systemic findings;
- governance-level reports.

Approval should not compromise auditor independence.

---

## 32. Report Distribution

Distribution should follow:

- need-to-know principles;
- sensitivity requirements;
- legal requirements;
- organisational policy;
- security controls.

Distribution lists should be controlled for material reports.

---

## 33. Report Integrity

Final reports should be protected from unauthorised modification.

The organisation should be able to distinguish:

- draft;
- reviewed;
- approved;
- superseded;
- final.

Material amendments should be controlled and traceable.

---

## 34. Audit Trail

The reporting process should preserve a connection between:

**Evidence → Testing → Finding → Conclusion → Report**

Material changes to findings or conclusions should be traceable.

---

## 35. Governance Assurance Limitations

Audit reporting should explicitly state where the audit cannot establish assurance.

Examples include:

- insufficient evidence;
- limited scope;
- untested environments;
- unavailable configuration information;
- incomplete records;
- unresolved contradictions;
- insufficient independence.

Absence of a finding does not necessarily prove absence of risk.

---

## 36. Relationship with Continuous Assurance

Audit is an independent assessment at defined points in time.

Continuous assurance provides ongoing confidence through monitoring, evidence and review.

Audit results should feed continuous assurance where appropriate.

Continuous assurance should not eliminate the need for independent audit.

---

## 37. Relationship with TEVV

TEVV establishes technical, operational, safety, security and mission-related evidence.

Audit assesses whether relevant governance requirements and controls are established, implemented and supported by appropriate evidence.

Audit should not replace technical TEVV.

---

## 38. Relationship with Incident Management

Incident investigations establish facts and causes associated with specific events.

Audit may subsequently assess whether governance and controls adequately address identified weaknesses.

Incident evidence should be considered where relevant to audit scope.

---

## 39. Relationship with Change Management

Material changes may trigger focused audit activity.

Audit may examine whether:

- changes were properly classified;
- approvals were obtained;
- configuration was controlled;
- testing occurred;
- revalidation was performed;
- reauthorisation occurred where required.

---

## 40. Relationship with Operational Authorisation

Audit findings may provide evidence relevant to continued authorisation.

However:

**Audit Assurance ≠ Operational Authorisation**

An audit conclusion should not be interpreted as permission to operate.

---

## 41. Governance Review

Governance authorities should periodically review audit results for:

- recurring weaknesses;
- systemic risks;
- assurance trends;
- control effectiveness;
- unresolved findings;
- emerging risks;
- resource requirements.

---

## 42. Metrics

Useful audit governance metrics may include:

- audit completion rate;
- finding rate;
- critical finding rate;
- overdue corrective actions;
- recurrence rate;
- time to closure;
- ineffective corrective-action rate;
- evidence-gap rate;
- systemic finding rate;
- audit coverage;
- assurance limitations.

Metrics should support decision-making rather than become targets that distort audit behaviour.

---

## 43. Learning

Audit results should generate organisational learning.

Learning may influence:

- policies;
- controls;
- TEVV methods;
- training;
- procurement;
- architecture;
- security;
- operational boundaries;
- authorisation conditions;
- assurance indicators.

---

## 44. Revalidation and Reauthorisation

Significant audit findings may require:

- additional assurance;
- focused TEVV;
- risk reassessment;
- revalidation;
- restriction;
- suspension;
- reauthorisation.

The response should be proportionate to consequence and residual uncertainty.

---

## 45. Reporting Records

The organisation should retain appropriate records of:

- audit reports;
- review comments;
- management responses;
- approvals;
- distributions;
- amendments;
- corrective actions;
- follow-up;
- closure.

These records support auditability and accountability.

---

## 46. Core Reporting Model

D-AIGAAF uses:

**Evidence → Finding → Risk → Conclusion → Action → Verification → Governance Decision**

This model ensures that audit reporting remains connected to governance action.

---

## 47. Core Rule

**Audit reporting shall communicate evidence, findings, uncertainty, conclusions and required action accurately and independently. Reports shall not overstate assurance, conceal material limitations, or convert audit conclusions into operational authority.**

---

## 48. Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Audit Planning → Criteria → Testing → Findings → Corrective Action → Verification → Closure → Governance Assurance**
