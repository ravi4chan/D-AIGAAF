# 00 — Workforce Governance Model

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document establishes the workforce governance model for D-AIGAAF.

Its purpose is to ensure that personnel responsible for AI capabilities have appropriate:

- roles;
- responsibilities;
- decision rights;
- authority;
- competence;
- AI literacy;
- capacity;
- independence;
- accountability.

Workforce governance applies across the AI lifecycle, from mission definition and acquisition through operational employment, continuous assurance, incident response, change, audit, and retirement.

The model is generic and unclassified and is intended to be adaptable to different defence organisations and AI capability types.

---

## 2. Workforce Governance Principles

D-AIGAAF applies the following principles:

1. **Clear Accountability** — Material responsibilities should have identifiable human ownership.
2. **Authority Must Match Responsibility** — Personnel should have sufficient authority to perform assigned responsibilities.
3. **Competence Proportionality** — Competence requirements should reflect role, consequence, complexity, and autonomy.
4. **Human Authority** — Operational authority remains assigned to authorised humans.
5. **Independence** — Assurance and audit functions should have sufficient independence to challenge.
6. **Traceability** — Material decisions and responsibilities should be reconstructable.
7. **Continuity** — Critical workforce functions should not depend on a single individual.
8. **Learning** — Workforce capability should improve through experience, testing, incidents, and audit.
9. **Adaptability** — Workforce arrangements should evolve with AI capability and operational conditions.
10. **No Governance by Assumption** — Technical capability does not establish authority, competence, or readiness.

---

## 3. Workforce Governance Architecture

The workforce governance architecture should establish clear relationships between:

**Role → Responsibility → Authority → Competence → Evidence → Accountability**

The organisation should determine, for each consequential AI capability:

- who owns the mission need;
- who owns the AI capability;
- who owns risk;
- who develops or acquires the capability;
- who performs TEVV;
- who provides assurance;
- who authorises operation;
- who supervises employment;
- who can intervene or override;
- who responds to incidents;
- who approves changes;
- who performs independent audit;
- who approves retirement.

Organisations may combine roles where appropriate, provided conflicts, independence, workload, and competence are adequately controlled.

---

## 4. Human Authority and High-Consequence AI

For consequential AI, human authority must remain explicit, bounded, competent, and traceable.

The organisation should identify the human authority responsible for decisions that may materially affect:

- human life;
- safety;
- property;
- mission outcomes;
- operational security;
- legal or policy obligations.

AI does not acquire authority merely through:

- technical capability;
- automation;
- connectivity;
- system state;
- interaction with another AI;
- portfolio inclusion;
- loss of communications;
- continuity mechanisms.

The level of workforce competence, supervision, independence, and oversight should increase where consequence or autonomy increases.

The core authorisation object remains:

**AI Capability × Mission × Environment × Autonomy × Human Authority**

---

## 5. Roles, Competence and Independence

Workforce roles should be defined according to the functions required by the capability.

Relevant functions may include:

- mission ownership;
- capability ownership;
- risk management;
- data governance;
- AI development;
- AI security;
- TEVV;
- assurance;
- operational authorisation;
- operational employment;
- incident response;
- change management;
- audit;
- maintenance;
- training.

Competence should cover the knowledge and skills necessary to perform each function.

Depending on role, this may include:

- operational knowledge;
- AI literacy;
- technical knowledge;
- risk management;
- security;
- assurance;
- human factors;
- legal and policy awareness.

Independent assurance and audit should be sufficiently separated from activities being assessed.

Where full separation is impractical, compensating controls should be established.

---

## 6. Capacity, Continuity and Human Performance

Workforce governance should address whether sufficient personnel and capacity exist to operate and govern AI safely.

Considerations should include:

- staffing;
- workload;
- fatigue;
- decision tempo;
- monitoring burden;
- intervention demands;
- specialist availability;
- succession;
- key-person dependency;
- incident-response capacity.

A capability should not depend on workforce assumptions that cannot realistically be sustained.

Particular attention should be given to human factors affecting meaningful human control, including:

- automation bias;
- cognitive overload;
- reduced situational awareness;
- excessive alert volume;
- insufficient time to intervene.

Critical workforce functions should have continuity and succession arrangements.

---

## 7. Competence, Training and Learning

Personnel should receive role-appropriate training and maintain competence throughout the lifecycle.

Training may include:

- AI fundamentals;
- system capabilities and limitations;
- uncertainty;
- autonomy;
- human-AI interaction;
- intervention and override;
- security;
- incident response;
- operational environment;
- change management;
- assurance responsibilities.

Competence should be assessed through appropriate evidence, which may include:

- qualifications;
- training;
- exercises;
- assessments;
- supervised experience;
- operational performance.

Lessons from audits, incidents, TEVV, exercises, and operational experience should feed workforce development.

---

## 8. Workforce Risk and Assurance

Workforce weaknesses should be treated as potential AI governance risks.

Examples include:

- inadequate competence;
- insufficient staffing;
- excessive workload;
- unclear authority;
- inadequate supervision;
- weak independence;
- single-person dependency;
- insufficient succession;
- loss of critical knowledge.

Workforce indicators may include:

- qualification status;
- training currency;
- staffing capacity;
- workload;
- exercise performance;
- intervention performance;
- incident-response readiness;
- recurring competence gaps.

Material workforce weaknesses should feed:

**Risk Assessment → Assurance → Corrective Action → Revalidation/Reauthorisation**

Where workforce limitations mean that authorised human control cannot realistically be maintained, continued operation should be reassessed.

## 10. Core Rule and Golden Thread

### Core Rule

**No consequential AI capability should depend on personnel who lack the authority, competence, capacity, independence, or situational understanding required to govern, assure, authorise, supervise, or safely employ it. Human authority must remain explicit, bounded, proportionate to consequence, and traceable.**

### Workforce Governance Cycle

**Define Roles → Assign Authority → Establish Competence → Qualify → Train → Exercise → Monitor → Review → Learn → Improve**

### Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Workforce Roles → Competence → Human Authority → Accountability**
