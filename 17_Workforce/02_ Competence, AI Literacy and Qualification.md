# 02 — Competence, AI Literacy and Qualification

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document establishes the D-AIGAAF requirements for workforce competence, AI literacy, qualification, and currency.

The objective is to ensure that personnel performing material AI governance, assurance, authorisation, technical, security, or operational functions possess knowledge and skills appropriate to:

- their role;
- the AI capability;
- the operational context;
- the level of risk;
- the level of autonomy;
- the consequences of failure.

Competence requirements should be proportionate rather than identical across all personnel.

---

## 2. Competence Framework

D-AIGAAF defines competence through:

**Knowledge + Skills + Experience + Judgement + Demonstrated Performance**

Competence should be assessed against the actual responsibilities of the role.

Relevant competence domains may include:

- operational/domain knowledge;
- AI and machine-learning concepts;
- AI limitations and uncertainty;
- risk management;
- security;
- data;
- TEVV;
- assurance;
- human factors;
- autonomy;
- legal and policy requirements;
- incident response;
- change management.

A qualification or training certificate alone should not automatically be treated as evidence of complete competence.

---

## 3. AI Literacy

Personnel who materially interact with AI should have AI literacy appropriate to their role.

At an appropriate level, this should include understanding of:

- what the AI is intended to do;
- what it is not intended to do;
- how outputs are generated at a conceptual level;
- known limitations;
- uncertainty;
- data dependencies;
- common failure modes;
- potential bias;
- automation bias;
- applicable autonomy;
- human-control mechanisms;
- escalation requirements.

AI literacy should enable personnel to make appropriately informed decisions without requiring every user to become an AI specialist.

Competence requirements should be defined for each material workforce role.

Examples include:

## 5. Qualification and Designation

Where the consequence of a function warrants it, organisations should establish formal qualification or designation requirements.

Qualification may require:

- prerequisite knowledge;
- formal training;
- examination or assessment;
- supervised experience;
- practical demonstration;
- scenario-based evaluation;
- documented approval.

Designation should specify:

- role;
- scope;
- authority;
- limitations;
- validity;
- renewal requirements.

Personnel should not exercise material authority outside their approved qualification or delegation.

---

## 6. Competence Demonstration and Assessment

Competence should be demonstrated through evidence appropriate to the role.

Possible evidence includes:

- written assessments;
- practical demonstrations;
- exercises;
- simulations;
- supervised employment;
- technical reviews;
- incident-response exercises;
- TEVV participation;
- performance assessments.

Assessment should test judgement where appropriate, not merely factual recall.

For high-consequence AI, competence assessment should include the ability to respond appropriately to:

- uncertainty;
- unexpected AI behaviour;
- degraded conditions;
- loss of communications;
- autonomy transitions;
- security events;
- conflicting information;
- time pressure.

---

## 7. Currency, Refresher Training and Change

Competence should remain current throughout the AI lifecycle.

Refresher training or reassessment may be required following:

- significant system changes;
- model updates;
- changes in autonomy;
- new operational environments;
- significant incidents;
- new threats;
- changes in procedures;
- extended periods without relevant activity;
- changes in role.

Training requirements should therefore connect to Module 15 change governance.

Where competence is no longer adequate, authority or operational responsibilities should be restricted until appropriate competence is restored.

---

## 8. Competence Risk and Assurance

Organisations should monitor competence risks that could weaken governance or meaningful human control.

Potential indicators include:

- expired qualifications;
- incomplete training;
- insufficient staffing;
- excessive workload;
- poor exercise performance;
- repeated operator errors;
- inadequate intervention performance;
- specialist shortages;
- key-person dependency.

Where a competence gap materially affects:

- human control;
- autonomy;
- safety;
- security;
- assurance;
- operational readiness;

the issue should enter the appropriate risk and assurance process.

Material competence deficiencies may require:

- additional training;
- increased supervision;
- reduced autonomy;
- restricted employment;
- additional assurance;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

## 10. Core Rule and Golden Thread

### Core Rule

**Personnel performing consequential AI functions shall possess competence appropriate to their role, authority, consequence, and level of autonomy. Qualification, training, and AI literacy shall be supported by evidence of demonstrated capability and maintained through appropriate currency and reassessment.**

### Competence Model

**Define Role → Identify Competence → Train → Assess → Qualify → Exercise → Maintain Currency → Monitor → Reassess → Improve**

### Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Workforce Role → Competence → Qualification → Effective Human Authority**
