# 04 — Workforce Capacity, Workload and Continuity

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document establishes requirements for ensuring that sufficient workforce capacity, sustainable workload, and continuity arrangements exist to support the safe governance and employment of AI capabilities.

Workforce capacity is treated as a governance and operational assurance factor.

A technically capable AI capability should not be considered ready for use if the organisation cannot sustain the human functions required to govern, supervise, assure, secure, maintain, and respond to it.

---

## 2. Workforce Capacity Requirements

Organisations should determine workforce capacity requirements based on:

- number and complexity of AI capabilities;
- mission criticality;
- consequence of failure;
- autonomy;
- operating tempo;
- environmental conditions;
- monitoring requirements;
- incident-response requirements;
- change frequency;
- assurance requirements;
- specialist dependencies.

Capacity should be assessed for both routine and abnormal conditions.

The relevant question is not simply whether personnel exist, but whether sufficient competent personnel are available when required.

---

## 3. Workload and Human Performance

Workload should be assessed for personnel responsible for AI governance and employment.

Relevant factors include:

- number of systems supervised;
- decision frequency;
- alert volume;
- monitoring burden;
- intervention frequency;
- cognitive load;
- time pressure;
- fatigue;
- shift duration;
- competing responsibilities.

Excessive workload may degrade:

- situational awareness;
- judgement;
- intervention performance;
- decision quality;
- meaningful human control.

Where workload materially affects control, the organisation should apply appropriate mitigation.

---

## 4. Operational Tempo and Surge Capacity

Workforce requirements may change during periods of increased operational activity.

Organisations should consider surge requirements for:

- AI operations;
- technical support;
- security;
- TEVV;
- assurance;
- incident response;
- maintenance;
- authorisation;
- change management.

Surge plans should identify available personnel, specialist support, escalation paths, and decision authority.

A temporary increase in operational tempo should not silently eliminate required governance controls.

---

## 5. Continuity of Critical Functions

Critical workforce functions should have continuity arrangements.

Continuity planning should address:

- absence;
- illness;
- rotation;
- reassignment;
- turnover;
- operational surge;
- incident response;
- loss of key personnel;
- loss of supporting organisations.

Critical functions may include:

- operational decision authority;
- AI supervision;
- security;
- incident response;
- technical support;
- assurance;
- authorisation.

Continuity arrangements should preserve competence and authority.

---

## 6. Key-Person Dependency and Succession

Organisations should identify roles where critical capability depends heavily on one individual.

Potential mitigations include:

- cross-training;
- documented procedures;
- succession planning;
- role redundancy;
- knowledge repositories;
- deputy arrangements;
- independent review.

Transfer of responsibility should preserve relevant knowledge concerning:

- risk;
- assumptions;
- limitations;
- configuration;
- authorisation conditions;
- incidents;
- unresolved findings;
- assurance status.

---

## 7. Degraded Conditions and Workforce Continuity

Workforce continuity should account for degraded organisational or operational conditions.

Examples include:

- communications degradation;
- disconnected operations;
- reduced technical support;
- limited specialist availability;
- infrastructure disruption;
- incident conditions.

The organisation should identify which human functions remain essential and how authority is maintained when normal support arrangements are unavailable.

Continuity arrangements must not create uncontrolled transfer of operational authority.

---

## 8. Capacity Monitoring and Assurance

Workforce capacity should be monitored using appropriate indicators.

Possible indicators include:

- staffing levels;
- qualification coverage;
- workload;
- overtime or extended duty;
- response times;
- intervention performance;
- training currency;
- specialist availability;
- incident-response readiness;
- unfilled critical roles.

Material deterioration should trigger appropriate governance action.

Possible responses include:

- additional staffing;
- workload reduction;
- increased supervision;
- restricted use;
- reduced autonomy;
- additional assurance;
- suspension.

---

## 9. Records, Review and Integration

Organisations should maintain appropriate records of:

- workforce requirements;
- staffing assumptions;
- role coverage;
- workload assessments;
- continuity arrangements;
- succession plans;
- capacity indicators;
- identified gaps;
- mitigations;
- governance decisions.

Workforce capacity should be reviewed following:

- significant AI changes;
- changes in autonomy;
- major incidents;
- changes in mission;
- significant environmental changes;
- sustained changes in operational tempo.

## 10. Core Rule and Golden Thread

### Core Rule

**AI capability should not be considered operationally sustainable where the organisation lacks sufficient competent personnel to maintain required human authority, supervision, intervention, assurance, security, maintenance, and incident response. Workforce capacity and workload are therefore part of operational readiness and continuing assurance.**

### Workforce Capacity Model

**Assess Demand → Determine Capacity → Identify Gaps → Mitigate → Monitor → Exercise → Review → Adapt**

### Golden Thread

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Workforce Capacity → Workload → Continuity → Sustained Human Control**
