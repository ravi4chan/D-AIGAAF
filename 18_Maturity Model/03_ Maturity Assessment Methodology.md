# 03-Maturity Assessment Methodology

## 1. Purpose and Scope

The D-AIGAAF Maturity Assessment Methodology defines a consistent, evidence-based approach for determining the maturity of AI governance capability within a defined assessment scope.

The methodology may be applied at organisational, programme, capability, mission, system, or process level. The assessment scope, assessment period, applicable domains, and intended use of results shall be established before assessment begins.

## 2. Assessment Principles

Maturity assessments shall be:

- evidence-based;
- proportionate to consequence and risk;
- context-sensitive;
- repeatable;
- transparent;
- sufficiently independent;
- focused on effectiveness rather than documentation alone;
- explicit about uncertainty and evidence limitations; and
- oriented toward improvement.

An assessment shall not treat the presence of policies or procedures as proof that they are effective in practice.

## 3. Assessment Scope and Context

Before assessment, the assessor shall define:

1. the organisation, programme, capability, or process being assessed;
2. the AI capabilities and lifecycle stages within scope;
3. relevant missions and operational contexts;
4. applicable maturity domains and dimensions;
5. assessment period;
6. relevant consequence and risk factors;
7. applicable dependencies and external parties; and
8. intended governance use of the results.

Changes to scope during assessment shall be documented and controlled.

## 4. Assessment Criteria

Each applicable maturity domain shall be assessed against defined criteria describing characteristics of the five maturity levels:

**Initial → Developing → Defined → Managed → Institutionalised**

Criteria should address progressively stronger evidence of:

- governance definition;
- implementation;
- consistency;
- effectiveness;
- measurement;
- integration;
- resilience;
- independent challenge; and
- continuous improvement.

Criteria shall be sufficiently specific to support consistent assessment while remaining adaptable to different organisational contexts.

## 5. Evidence Collection

Assessors shall collect evidence appropriate to the assessment scope.

Evidence may include:

- policies and governance documents;
- roles and decision-rights records;
- risk assessments;
- requirements and control records;
- data and model governance records;
- security assessments;
- TEVV evidence;
- operational authorisation records;
- employment and monitoring records;
- incident and change records;
- audit results;
- workforce competence records;
- supplier assurance evidence; and
- lessons-learned and improvement records.

Evidence should demonstrate not only that a process exists, but that it has been implemented and produces the intended governance outcome.

## 6. Assessment Execution

A typical assessment sequence is:

**Scope → Context → Criteria → Evidence → Evaluation → Scoring → Confidence → Findings → Review → Reporting**

For each applicable domain, assessors should:

1. review the relevant criteria;
2. examine available evidence;
3. determine the demonstrated capability;
4. identify weaknesses and inconsistencies;
5. assess the effectiveness of implementation;
6. determine the appropriate maturity level;
7. record confidence and evidence limitations; and
8. document material findings.

Where evidence supports different maturity levels across sub-capabilities, the assessment should preserve that distinction rather than averaging away a material weakness.

## 7. Assessment of Effectiveness

Maturity shall be based on demonstrated capability, not solely on documented intent.

Assessors should distinguish between:

- **Documented** — the process is described.
- **Implemented** — the process is being used.
- **Effective** — the process achieves its intended outcome.
- **Measured** — performance is monitored.
- **Improved** — evidence shows learning and systematic enhancement.

Higher maturity levels require progressively stronger evidence across these characteristics.

## 8. Independence, Challenge and Quality Control

Assessments of consequential AI governance should include an appropriate level of assessor independence and challenge.

Independence may involve:

- separation from the activity being assessed;
- independent review of significant findings;
- challenge of unsupported maturity claims;
- conflict-of-interest management; and
- escalation of unresolved disagreements.

Assessment quality shall be reviewed before results are used for significant governance decisions.

## 9. Findings and Assessment Output

An assessment should produce, at minimum:

- defined scope and context;
- assessed domains and dimensions;
- maturity level for each applicable area;
- supporting evidence;
- confidence assessment;
- material gaps and weaknesses;
- cross-domain dependencies;
- priority risks;
- recommended improvement actions; and
- governance decisions or follow-up requirements.

Results should make critical weaknesses visible even when aggregate maturity appears strong.

## 10. Core Rule and Assessment Logic

**Core Rule:**
> A maturity level shall be assigned only to the extent that the required capability is demonstrated by credible evidence appropriate to the assessment context.

The methodology shall avoid the assumption that:

**Higher Maturity = Safer AI = Authorised AI**

Instead:

**Maturity Evidence → Governance Capability Assessment → Gap and Risk Identification → Improvement → Reassessment**

Maturity assessment shall remain integrated with the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need → Risk → Requirements → Controls → Testing → Evidence → Assurance → Authority → Conditions → Boundaries → Employment → Monitoring → Change/Incident → Learning → Revalidation/Reauthorisation → Workforce → Maturity → Continuous Improvement**
