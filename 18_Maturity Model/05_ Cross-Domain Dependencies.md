# 05-Maturity Gaps and Risk Prioritisation

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document defines how D-AIGAAF maturity gaps shall be identified, evaluated, and prioritised according to their potential effect on AI governance, assurance, authorisation, operational employment, and mission outcomes.

A maturity gap is a difference between the demonstrated governance capability and the capability required for the defined context, consequence level, or target state.

## 2. Identifying Maturity Gaps

Gaps may arise from:

- absent governance processes;
- incomplete implementation;
- inconsistent application;
- ineffective controls;
- insufficient evidence;
- inadequate competence or capacity;
- weak independence or accountability;
- unmanaged dependencies;
- outdated processes;
- poor integration between governance domains; or
- failure to adapt to changing missions, technology, threats, or environments.

Each material gap should be linked to the relevant maturity criterion and evidence.

## 3. Gap Classification

Gaps should be classified according to their nature, including:

| Gap Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Capability Gap | Required governance capability does not exist or is insufficient. |
| Implementation Gap | Defined capability exists but is not consistently implemented. |
| Effectiveness Gap | Process or control exists but does not reliably achieve its intended outcome. |
| Evidence Gap | Capability may exist but cannot be adequately demonstrated. |
| Integration Gap | Governance elements exist but do not operate coherently across domains. |
| Resilience Gap | Capability may function normally but is insufficient under disruption, degradation, or adversarial conditions. |
| Sustainability Gap | Governance depends excessively on individuals, temporary arrangements, or fragile resources. |

Multiple classifications may apply to a single finding.

## 4. Risk-Based Prioritisation

Maturity gaps shall be prioritised using more than the size of the maturity difference.

Priority should consider:

**Consequence → Risk → Exposure → Dependency → Control Weakness → Evidence Confidence → Operational Impact**

Factors may include:

- potential harm to people;
- mission impact;
- legal or policy consequences;
- security implications;
- autonomy level;
- reversibility of actions;
- environmental uncertainty;
- scale of affected operations;
- dependency criticality; and
- likelihood of the gap being exploited or becoming consequential.

## 5. Critical Governance Gaps

A gap may be considered critical where it materially undermines the organisation's ability to govern or control a consequential AI capability.

Examples include:

- unclear accountability for consequential decisions;
- inadequate human authority or intervention capability;
- inability to establish required assurance evidence;
- uncontrolled autonomy;
- material AI security weaknesses;
- inability to operate safely in the intended environment;
- uncontrolled configuration or change;
- inability to detect or respond to serious incidents; or
- dependence on unavailable or inadequately controlled external capabilities.

Critical gaps shall receive explicit governance attention regardless of overall maturity.

## 6. Gap Severity

A common severity model may be used:

| Severity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Low | Limited governance weakness with minimal immediate consequence. |
| Moderate | Material weakness requiring planned corrective action. |
| High | Significant weakness that may undermine governance effectiveness or create material risk. |
| Critical | Weakness that may prevent safe, controlled, assured, or appropriately authorised operation. |

Severity shall be determined in context and shall not be inferred solely from the maturity score.

## 7. Risk Treatment and Corrective Action

For each material gap, the responsible authority should determine an appropriate treatment, such as:

- remediate;
- strengthen existing controls;
- obtain additional evidence;
- increase oversight;
- restrict scope;
- reduce autonomy;
- impose additional operational conditions;
- defer deployment or employment;
- suspend affected activity; or
- accept residual risk where authorised under applicable governance arrangements.

Corrective actions shall have defined ownership, expected outcomes, target dates, and verification arrangements.

## 8. Cross-Domain Dependencies

Maturity gaps shall be assessed for dependencies across domains.

For example, a workforce competence gap may affect human control, TEVV, operational employment, and authorisation. A data provenance weakness may affect model assurance, security, mission effectiveness, and continuous assurance.

Cross-domain dependencies shall be recorded so that corrective action addresses root causes rather than isolated symptoms.

## 9. Monitoring and Escalation

Material maturity gaps shall be monitored until closure or formally accepted disposition.

Escalation should occur where:

- corrective action is overdue;
- risk increases;
- the gap affects a consequential capability;
- evidence confidence deteriorates;
- a related incident occurs;
- operational conditions change; or
- the gap affects an authorisation condition or boundary.

Closure shall require evidence that the intended improvement has been implemented and is effective.

## 10. Core Rule and Prioritisation Logic

**Core Rule:**
> Maturity gaps shall be prioritised according to their potential consequences and governance significance, not merely according to the numerical difference between current and target maturity.

The prioritisation logic is:

**Current Capability → Required Capability → Gap → Consequence/Risk → Priority → Treatment → Verification → Reassessment**

The objective is not simply to raise maturity scores. It is to eliminate weaknesses that could undermine the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread and the organisation's ability to govern AI safely, accountably, and effectively.
