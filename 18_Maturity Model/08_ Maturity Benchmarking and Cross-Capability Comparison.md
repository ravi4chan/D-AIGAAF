# 08-Maturity Benchmarking and Cross-Capability Comparison

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document defines principles for benchmarking and comparing D-AIGAAF maturity across organisations, programmes, capabilities, missions, systems, or governance domains.

Benchmarking is intended to identify relative strengths, weaknesses, patterns, and improvement opportunities. It shall not be used to imply that one AI capability is inherently safer or more suitable for operational use solely because it has a higher maturity score.

## 2. Benchmarking Principles

Benchmarking shall be:

- context-aware;
- evidence-based;
- transparent about methodology;
- based on comparable assessment scopes;
- sensitive to consequence and risk;
- explicit about uncertainty;
- resistant to misleading league-table comparisons; and
- focused on learning and improvement.

Results from assessments using materially different scopes, criteria, evidence standards, or contexts should not be directly compared without appropriate qualification.

## 3. Comparable Assessment Units

Before comparison, the assessment units shall be defined.

Possible units include:

- organisations;
- governance functions;
- programmes;
- AI capabilities;
- missions;
- lifecycle stages;
- maturity domains; or
- individual processes.

The comparison should specify whether it concerns governance maturity, implementation effectiveness, resilience, institutionalisation, or another defined characteristic.

## 4. Normalisation and Context

Benchmarking should account for differences in:

- mission consequence;
- autonomy;
- operational environment;
- organisational scale;
- AI lifecycle stage;
- regulatory and policy context;
- workforce size and competence;
- supplier dependencies;
- technology complexity;
- threat environment; and
- assessment maturity.

A higher score in a lower-consequence context should not automatically be interpreted as stronger governance than a lower score in a significantly more demanding context.

## 5. Domain-Level Comparison

Cross-capability comparison should prioritise domain-level patterns rather than a single aggregate score.

Useful comparisons may include:

| Comparison | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Domain maturity | Identify relative governance strengths and weaknesses. |
| Evidence confidence | Determine reliability of comparative results. |
| Critical gaps | Identify weaknesses requiring priority action. |
| Target vs current state | Measure improvement requirements. |
| Trend over time | Determine whether governance capability is improving or degrading. |
| Cross-domain dependencies | Identify systemic weaknesses. |

Critical weaknesses shall remain visible even when aggregate maturity appears strong.

## 6. Internal Benchmarking

Internal benchmarking may compare:

- programmes within an organisation;
- AI capabilities across missions;
- governance functions;
- business or operational units;
- lifecycle stages; or
- maturity results over time.

Internal comparisons should use consistent criteria and assessment methods where possible.

The primary purpose should be to identify transferable good practice and address systemic weaknesses rather than create competition based solely on maturity scores.

## 7. External Benchmarking

External benchmarking may use published frameworks, peer assessments, industry practices, standards, or appropriately shared assessment results.

External comparisons shall document:

- source and methodology;
- assessment date;
- scope;
- evidence basis;
- maturity definitions;
- limitations; and
- material contextual differences.

External maturity claims shall not be treated as equivalent to D-AIGAAF assessments unless methodological comparability has been established.

## 8. Benchmarking Risks

Benchmarking can create misleading incentives if maturity scores become organisational targets without regard to governance outcomes.

Potential risks include:

- score optimisation instead of capability improvement;
- under-reporting weaknesses;
- inappropriate aggregation;
- false confidence;
- ignoring contextual differences;
- competitive pressure affecting assessor independence; and
- treating maturity as a proxy for safety or authorisation.

Governance processes should actively guard against these behaviours.

## 9. Reporting and Use of Comparative Results

Benchmarking reports should show:

- assessment scope;
- maturity by domain;
- evidence confidence;
- critical gaps;
- contextual factors;
- trends;
- target maturity;
- improvement priorities; and
- limitations on comparability.

Comparative results may support strategic planning, investment, assurance planning, workforce development, supplier management, and continuous improvement.

They shall not independently determine operational authorisation or risk acceptance.

## 10. Core Rule and Benchmarking Logic

**Core Rule:**
> Benchmarking shall compare governance capability only to the extent that assessment scope, evidence, methodology, and context are sufficiently comparable; a higher maturity score shall never be treated as automatic evidence of safer or authorised AI.

The benchmarking logic is:

**Define Scope → Establish Comparability → Normalise Context → Compare Evidence-Based Results → Identify Patterns → Prioritise Improvement → Monitor Trends**

The objective is to convert comparison into organisational learning while preserving the D-AIGAAF distinction between:

**Maturity ≠ Safety**  
**Maturity ≠ Assurance**  
**Maturity ≠ Readiness**  
**Maturity ≠ Authorisation**
