# 07-Crosswalk to IndiaAI Governance Guidelines

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the relationship between D-AIGAAF — Defence AI Governance, Assurance & Operational Authorisation Framework and India's emerging **IndiaAI Governance Guidelines** and associated Government of India AI-governance work.

The purpose is to identify areas of alignment, distinguish policy guidance from binding law, and show where D-AIGAAF provides additional defence-specific governance mechanisms.

This crosswalk is intentionally separate from Serial 06, which addresses the broader **IndiaAI Responsible AI principles/framework**.

## 2. Scope

This document focuses on the Indian AI-governance guidance and institutional framework emerging through the IndiaAI Mission and Government of India processes, including:

- IndiaAI governance and guidelines development;
- the Government's work on safe and trusted AI;
- multi-stakeholder AI governance mechanisms;
- governance of AI risks and impacts;
- accountability and responsible deployment;
- Indian priorities around trustworthy, inclusive and human-centric AI.

It does not treat these materials as a substitute for:

- applicable Indian law;
- defence policy;
- technical standards;
- TEVV;
- AI security controls;
- operational authorisation; or
- mission-specific risk management.

## 3. Status of the Indian AI Governance Guidelines

India's AI-governance framework has been evolving through the IndiaAI Mission, MeitY and associated expert and advisory processes.

The Government constituted an advisory group and a subcommittee on AI governance and guidelines development. The subcommittee's work was intended to contribute to an approach for trustworthy and responsible AI in India.

Government material subsequently developed into the IndiaAI Governance Guidelines.

Because India's AI-governance architecture continues to evolve, D-AIGAAF treats the guidelines as a **living policy reference** and requires organisations to verify the latest applicable Government of India position before using the crosswalk for formal compliance purposes.

This distinction is essential:

> **Governance guidance is not automatically equivalent to legislation, regulation, a military directive, a technical standard or an operational authorisation.**

## 4. Why the Crosswalk Matters to D-AIGAAF

India's AI governance direction is particularly relevant to D-AIGAAF because the framework seeks to establish an Indian foundation for:

- responsible AI;
- safe and trusted AI;
- accountability;
- human-centric AI;
- risk management;
- transparency;
- innovation;
- governance capacity;
- AI testing and evaluation; and
- public trust.

D-AIGAAF uses this policy direction as an Indian alignment layer while extending governance into defence-specific questions:

**Who may authorise an AI capability?**

**Under what mission conditions?**

**At what autonomy level?**

**With what evidence?**

**Under whose human authority?**

**What happens when communications degrade, the environment changes or the system behaves unexpectedly?**

## 6. Governance and Accountability

Indian AI governance places significant importance on accountable and responsible development and deployment.

D-AIGAAF translates this into a formal governance structure covering:

- organisational accountability;
- governance bodies;
- decision rights;
- role separation;
- accountable authorities;
- human oversight;
- operational authorisation;
- audit;
- evidence;
- incident governance;
- workforce competence.

A central D-AIGAAF principle is:

> **Accountability must remain identifiable even when AI performs analysis, recommendation or autonomous functions.**

The framework therefore prevents responsibility from becoming ambiguous through phrases such as:

- “the system decided”;
- “the algorithm selected”;
- “the model recommended”; or
- “the AI acted”.

AI output may be part of a decision chain, but the accountable authority must remain identifiable.

## 7. Human-Centric Governance

India's AI-governance direction emphasises human-centred and responsible use of AI.

D-AIGAAF operationalises this through the Human Authority module.

It distinguishes:

- human presence;
- human oversight;
- human supervision;
- meaningful human control;
- intervention authority;
- override authority;
- accountability.

Human involvement is meaningful only where the authorised human has sufficient:

- authority;
- competence;
- information;
- situational awareness;
- time;
- ability to intervene; and
- ability to place the system into an appropriate safe state.

This becomes especially important as autonomy increases.

## 8. Risk-Based Governance

IndiaAI governance emphasises the need to address risks associated with AI.

D-AIGAAF applies a structured risk model in which governance intensity is influenced by:

- mission consequence;
- potential harm;
- autonomy;
- uncertainty;
- environmental conditions;
- security threats;
- human-control requirements;
- data quality;
- system dependencies.

D-AIGAAF therefore avoids a universal “AI risk” category.

Instead:

> **Risk is contextual, consequence-dependent and dynamic.**

The same AI capability can present materially different governance requirements when used for different missions or under different operating conditions.

## 9. Safety and Trusted AI

The IndiaAI governance direction includes safe and trusted AI as a major objective.

D-AIGAAF expands this objective into measurable governance mechanisms:

**Safety objective**
→ safety requirements

**Safety requirements**
→ controls

**Controls**
→ TEVV

**TEVV**
→ evidence

**Evidence**
→ assurance

**Assurance**
→ authorisation

**Authorisation**
→ controlled employment

**Employment**
→ continuous assurance

This prevents “safe and trusted AI” from becoming only a policy statement.

## 10. Testing, Evaluation and Governance

Government of India AI-governance work has increasingly recognised the importance of AI testing, evaluation and trustworthy deployment.

D-AIGAAF places TEVV at the centre of the governance lifecycle.

TEVV is used to evaluate, as applicable:

- performance;
- reliability;
- robustness;
- safety;
- security;
- adversarial resilience;
- human-AI interaction;
- autonomy;
- mission effectiveness;
- operational-environment performance;
- uncertainty;
- failure behaviour.

D-AIGAAF further requires TEVV evidence to feed operational decision-making.

A test result that is not connected to an authorisation or assurance decision has limited governance value.

## 11. Data Governance

Responsible AI governance depends heavily on trustworthy data.

D-AIGAAF incorporates Indian responsible-AI governance objectives through a dedicated Data & Information architecture covering:

- data ownership;
- data governance;
- quality;
- integrity;
- provenance;
- lineage;
- representativeness;
- distribution shift;
- drift;
- lifecycle management;
- retention;
- disposal;
- data incidents.

The framework recognises:

> **Poor data governance can become an AI safety, security and mission-risk problem.**

Data therefore remains part of the governance chain rather than being treated as a purely technical input.

## 12. Transparency and Documentation

IndiaAI governance places importance on transparency and accountable AI.

D-AIGAAF operationalises this through documentation and evidence requirements.

Relevant records may include:

- AI purpose;
- use case;
- system boundaries;
- assumptions;
- limitations;
- data sources;
- model information;
- configuration;
- test results;
- risk assessment;
- authorisation conditions;
- operational constraints;
- incidents;
- changes;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

Defence requires a controlled approach to transparency.

The framework therefore requires information to be available to the appropriate authorised stakeholders while protecting:

- classified or sensitive information;
- operational information;
- security-sensitive details;
- intellectual property;
- sensitive supplier information.

## 13. AI Governance in High-Consequence Environments

General AI governance guidance must be strengthened where AI can materially affect:

- human life;
- safety;
- security;
- critical infrastructure;
- military operations;
- national interests;
- significant resources.

D-AIGAAF therefore introduces **consequence-sensitive governance**.

Higher consequence should generally result in stronger requirements for:

- assurance;
- human authority;
- evidence;
- testing;
- security;
- monitoring;
- intervention;
- authorisation;
- reauthorisation.

This does not mean that every high-consequence system must be prohibited.

It means that the governance burden must be proportionate to the potential consequence.

## 14. Defence-Specific Operational Extension

IndiaAI governance guidance is necessarily broader than a defence operational governance framework.

D-AIGAAF therefore adds:

### 14.1 Operational Authorisation

A capability must receive explicit authority for its intended mission, environment and autonomy level.

### 14.2 Operational Employment

Governance continues after deployment and covers actual use.

### 14.3 Degraded and Disconnected Operations

The system must be assessed where communications, connectivity or supporting services are degraded or unavailable.

### 14.4 Adversarial Environment

The system must be evaluated against hostile or deliberately manipulated conditions.

### 14.5 Fail-Safe

Unexpected behaviour and loss of control require predefined protective responses.

### 14.6 Reauthorisation

Material changes or incidents may invalidate previous authorisation.

### 14.7 Human Authority

Operational decision rights remain explicitly assigned to humans.

## 16. Policy-to-Control Translation

D-AIGAAF recommends translating each relevant IndiaAI governance expectation through four questions:

### 16.1 What is the governance objective?

Example:

**Promote trustworthy AI.**

### 16.2 What requirement follows?

Example:

**The AI capability shall demonstrate reliability and defined limitations for its intended mission.**

### 16.3 What evidence demonstrates the requirement?

Example:

**TEVV results, risk assessment, operational trials and monitoring records.**

### 16.4 Who has authority to accept the residual risk?

Example:

**An explicitly designated and appropriately authorised human authority.**

This prevents high-level governance language from remaining disconnected from operational accountability.

## 17. Governance Evolution

India's AI governance ecosystem is continuing to develop.

D-AIGAAF is designed to accommodate this evolution through:

- policy alignment;
- crosswalk updates;
- governance reviews;
- change management;
- continuous assurance;
- legal and policy monitoring;
- revalidation;
- reauthorisation.

A change in Indian AI governance policy should trigger an assessment of whether affected D-AIGAAF controls, policies or evidence requirements need revision.

This does not mean that every policy update automatically changes operational authorisation.

The effect should be assessed through the D-AIGAAF change-governance process.

## 18. Evidence of Alignment

Organisations claiming alignment with relevant IndiaAI governance guidance should maintain evidence such as:

- AI governance policy;
- governance roles;
- accountability assignments;
- AI inventory;
- risk assessments;
- impact assessments where appropriate;
- data-governance documentation;
- TEVV evidence;
- security assessments;
- human-oversight design;
- AI literacy and competence records;
- operational limitations;
- authorisation decisions;
- monitoring results;
- incident records;
- change assessments;
- audit evidence.

The principle is:

> **Governance alignment should be demonstrable rather than merely asserted.**

## 19. Relationship to Indian Law

IndiaAI governance guidance should not be confused with statutory obligations.

Where an AI system processes personal data, operates within a regulated sector, or falls under another applicable legal or policy regime, those requirements must be independently assessed.

D-AIGAAF therefore maintains separate crosswalks for:

- Indian data-protection requirements;
- broader Indian AI policy;
- Indian standards and technical frameworks.

The framework also requires legal and policy requirements to be incorporated into mission-specific governance.

## 20. Limitations

This crosswalk does not claim:

- official endorsement of D-AIGAAF by IndiaAI or MeitY;
- that IndiaAI Governance Guidelines constitute a military authorisation regime;
- that policy guidance automatically creates legal obligations;
- that alignment demonstrates compliance;
- that governance guidance eliminates operational AI risk;
- that responsible AI principles alone establish technical assurance; or
- that a crosswalk substitutes for legal review or competent authority.

The document is an **alignment mechanism**.

## 21. Implementation Guidance

For an organisation implementing D-AIGAAF in India, IndiaAI governance guidance should be considered during:

1. AI governance-policy development.
2. AI strategy and portfolio governance.
3. Mission/use-case approval.
4. Risk assessment.
5. Data governance.
6. Human-authority design.
7. TEVV planning.
8. Operational authorisation.
9. Continuous assurance.
10. Governance review.

Relevant Indian legal and regulatory obligations should then be mapped separately.

## 22. Primary Government Reference Basis

The crosswalk should be maintained against current Government of India material, particularly:

- IndiaAI Mission materials;
- MeitY AI and Emerging Technologies Division material;
- IndiaAI Responsible AI and Safe & Trusted AI material;
- Government advisory and expert-group outputs concerning AI governance;
- IndiaAI Governance Guidelines and subsequent official updates.

Government material has described a multi-stakeholder approach to AI governance and the development of guidelines intended to support a trustworthy and accountable AI ecosystem.

Because this area is evolving, the latest official Government of India publications should take precedence over historical drafts or secondary summaries.

## 23. Final Crosswalk Position

IndiaAI governance guidance provides an important Indian policy foundation for responsible, safe, trustworthy and human-centred AI.

D-AIGAAF extends that foundation into the defence operational domain by introducing explicit mechanisms for:

**Mission → Consequence → Risk → Autonomy → Human Authority → TEVV → Security → Environment → Authorisation → Employment → Monitoring → Revalidation → Reauthorisation**

The resulting principle is:

> **IndiaAI governance establishes an important national responsible-AI direction; D-AIGAAF translates relevant governance objectives into defence-specific requirements, controls, evidence, authority and operational governance.**
