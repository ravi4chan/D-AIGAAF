# 18-Maturity Assessment Templates

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the template set for **D-AIGAAF Module 18 — Maturity Model**.

The D-AIGAAF maturity model provides a structured method for assessing how consistently and effectively an organisation can govern, assure, authorise and safely employ AI capabilities.

Maturity is intended to support:

- capability improvement;
- governance prioritisation;
- risk-informed investment;
- benchmarking;
- leadership visibility;
- implementation planning;
- continuous improvement.

The central principle is:

> **Maturity describes organisational capability and consistency; it does not by itself establish compliance, assurance, safety or operational authorisation.**

The maturity improvement cycle is:

**Assess → Evidence → Score → Identify Gaps → Prioritise Risk → Improve → Verify → Reassess → Institutionalise**

---

# 2. Fundamental Distinctions

D-AIGAAF explicitly distinguishes:

### Maturity

How developed, repeatable and effective the organisation's governance capability is.

### Compliance

Whether specified requirements have been met.

### Assurance

Whether sufficient evidence supports justified confidence in a capability.

### Authorisation

Whether an authorised authority has permitted a capability to operate under defined conditions.

These are related but not interchangeable.

> **High maturity does not automatically authorise a capability.**

A highly mature organisation can still prohibit deployment of a high-risk capability when evidence is insufficient.

# 6. Maturity Should Be Capability-Based

A maturity assessment should ask:

> **What can the organisation reliably do?**

rather than:

> **How many documents does the organisation possess?**

For example:

A policy may demonstrate intent.

A tested, repeatable governance process demonstrates capability.

Evidence of repeated effective execution provides stronger maturity evidence.

---

# 7. Template 18-003 — Maturity Level & Capability Stage Assessment

## Purpose

Assigns an overall maturity stage based on demonstrated organisational capability.

## Recommended Levels

### Level 0 — Unstructured

AI governance is largely absent, ad hoc or dependent on individual initiative.

### Level 1 — Initial

Basic awareness and isolated governance activities exist.

### Level 2 — Defined

Core processes, responsibilities and requirements have been documented.

### Level 3 — Implemented

Processes are operating across relevant AI capabilities with defined evidence and accountability.

### Level 4 — Managed

Performance is measured, risks are actively managed, assurance is integrated and governance is consistently applied.

### Level 5 — Adaptive

The organisation continuously learns, anticipates emerging risks, adapts controls and integrates governance across the AI ecosystem.

## Important

These levels describe organisational capability, not a safety rating for an individual AI system.

---

| Level | Primary Characteristic |
|---|---|
| 0 | Absent / ad hoc |

# 9. Template 18-004 — Maturity Assessment Methodology Record

## Purpose

Defines how an assessment is conducted.

## Assessment Steps

**1. Define Scope**

Identify organisation, capability, mission and governance domains.

**2. Establish Criteria**

Define the expected characteristics for each maturity level.

**3. Collect Evidence**

Gather relevant records, observations, interviews, tests and operational evidence.

**4. Assess Capability**

Determine what the organisation can demonstrably perform.

**5. Score**

Assign maturity based on evidence.

**6. Challenge**

Conduct independent review where appropriate.

**7. Identify Gaps**

Compare current and target states.

**8. Prioritise**

Rank gaps by consequence, risk and strategic importance.

**9. Improve**

Implement corrective and improvement actions.

**10. Reassess**

Verify progress and update maturity.

---

# 10. Evidence Hierarchy for Maturity

Evidence may include:

- policy;
- governance records;
- implemented procedures;
- decision records;
- training records;
- TEVV evidence;
- audit findings;
- operational records;
- incident history;
- monitoring data;
- exercise results;
- independent assessments;
- demonstrated outcomes.

A documented process without evidence of implementation should receive limited maturity credit.

- Score
- Confidence

- Reviewer

## Scoring Principle

Scores should be based on demonstrated capability rather than optimism or organisational aspiration.

Where evidence is insufficient:

**Score confidence should decrease.**

Uncertainty should be visible.

---

# 12. Avoiding False Precision

A maturity score should not create artificial certainty.

For example:

**3.74 / 5**

may suggest greater precision than the evidence supports.

Where appropriate, use:

- maturity level;
- confidence;
- evidence quality;
- range;
- domain-specific qualification.

Example:

**Level 3 — Implemented, moderate confidence**

is often more informative than an unexplained numerical score.

# 18. Maturity and Assurance

Maturity can support assurance but does not replace it.

For example:

A mature organisation may have excellent:

- governance;
- documentation;
- TEVV;
- audit;
- workforce;
- monitoring.

But an individual AI capability may still lack sufficient evidence for operational authorisation.

Therefore:

> **Organisational maturity is an enabling condition, not proof of capability-level assurance.**

---

# 19. Maturity and Operational Authorisation

Maturity should never become an automatic authorisation mechanism.

The correct relationship is:

**Maturity**
→ supports governance capability

**Capability Evidence**
→ supports assurance

**Risk Assessment**
→ determines risk

**Operational Authority**
→ makes the authorisation decision

A high maturity score cannot override inadequate capability-specific evidence.

Do not ask only:

> “Who has the higher maturity score?”

> “Why is the score different, and does the difference matter for the risks each organisation faces?”

Context is essential.

# 23. Maturity and Organisational Learning

Higher maturity should increasingly demonstrate the ability to learn from:

- incidents;
- audit;
- TEVV;
- operational employment;
- exercises;
- changes;
- near misses;
- supplier events;
- emerging threats.

The key question is:

> **Does the organisation systematically convert experience into improved governance and operational capability?**

---

# 24. Maturity and Evidence of Effectiveness

A mature process should increasingly demonstrate not just:

**Process exists**

but:

**Process operates**

and then:

**Process is effective**

and ultimately:

**Process adapts based on evidence and experience**

This represents a progression from documentation to institutional capability.

---

# 25. Maturity and AI Security

Security maturity should include the ability to:

- identify AI-specific threats;
- protect models and data;
- secure instructions and tools;
- control privileges;
- detect adversarial behaviour;
- conduct red teaming;
- respond to incidents;
- manage dependencies;
- reassess security after change.

Conventional cybersecurity maturity alone should not be assumed to represent AI security maturity.

---

# 26. Maturity and Human Authority

Human-control maturity should consider whether the organisation can consistently establish:

- clear authority;
- competent personnel;
- sufficient information;
- realistic intervention;
- appropriate workload;
- effective override;
- accountability;
- escalation.

A policy requiring human oversight is insufficient if the organisation cannot operationally provide it.

---

# 27. Maturity and Degraded Operations

For defence AI, maturity should account for the ability to govern and safely operate capabilities under:

- degraded communications;
- disconnected operation;
- adversarial conditions;
- incomplete information;
- contested electromagnetic environments;
- rapidly changing missions;
- loss of supporting infrastructure.

A governance process that works only in ideal conditions may not represent sufficient operational maturity.

---

# 28. Maturity and Change

A mature organisation should detect that governance requirements can change even when software does not.

Examples:

- new mission;
- new geography;
- new threat;
- new operator;
- new data;
- new supplier;
- new autonomy mode;
- new communications environment.

Maturity therefore includes the ability to recognise context change and trigger appropriate reassessment.

---

# 29. Anti-Pattern — Maturity as a Compliance Badge

D-AIGAAF rejects:

> “We are Level 5, therefore our AI is safe.”

Maturity is not a safety certification.

It describes organisational capability.

Capability-specific risk, evidence, assurance and authorisation remain necessary.

---

# 30. Anti-Pattern — Document Count as Maturity

More policies, registers and templates do not automatically indicate greater maturity.

An organisation with:

- fewer;
- clearer;
- implemented;
- tested;
- effective

controls may be more mature than an organisation with hundreds of unused documents.

The objective is **governance capability**, not documentation volume.

---

# 31. Anti-Pattern — One Score for Everything

A single organisational score can conceal critical weaknesses.

For example:

**Overall Level 4**

may hide:

- Security — Level 2
- Human Authority — Level 2
- TEVV — Level 3
- Governance — Level 5

Domain-level assessment is therefore essential.

Critical weaknesses should remain visible even when overall maturity is high.

---

# 32. Anti-Pattern — Maturity Without Risk

Not every maturity gap matters equally.

A lower maturity level in a low-consequence administrative AI use case may be acceptable.

The same maturity gap in a highly autonomous, safety-critical operational capability may be unacceptable.

Maturity improvement should therefore be risk-informed.

---

# 33. Maturity Dashboard Principles

Where dashboards are used, display at least:

- maturity by domain;
- target maturity;
- evidence confidence;
- critical gaps;
- high-risk weaknesses;
- improvement status;
- overdue actions;
- recurring findings;
- trend.

The dashboard should support decisions, not replace detailed evidence.

---

# 34. Maturity Assessment Frequency

Frequency should reflect:

- organisational change;
- AI portfolio change;
- risk;
- incidents;
- regulatory change;
- threat evolution;
- previous assessment findings.

A major organisational or technological change may justify reassessment before the normal review cycle.

---

# 35. Maturity Assessment Independence

Higher-consequence assessments may benefit from:

- independent assessors;
- peer review;
- internal audit;
- external assessment;
- governance committee challenge.

Independence requirements should be proportionate to the consequence and purpose of the assessment.

---

# 36. Maturity and Portfolio Governance

Maturity assessment can support portfolio-level decisions such as:

- where to invest;
- where to strengthen controls;
- which capabilities require deeper assurance;
- where common weaknesses exist;
- where shared infrastructure creates systemic risk.

Portfolio maturity should not obscure capability-specific risk.

---

# 37. Maturity and Systemic Risk

Some weaknesses can affect many AI capabilities simultaneously.

Examples:

- inadequate identity and access management;
- weak model provenance;
- poor configuration control;
- inadequate AI literacy;
- insufficient TEVV capacity;
- weak incident response;
- uncontrolled supplier updates.

Such gaps may deserve higher priority because of their portfolio-wide consequences.

---

# 38. Maturity Golden Thread Integration

The Maturity Model connects organisational improvement to the D-AIGAAF Golden Thread:

**Mission Need**
→ **Risk**
→ **Requirements**
→ **Controls**
→ **Testing**
→ **Evidence**
→ **Assurance**
→ **Authority**
→ **Conditions**
→ **Boundaries**
→ **Employment**
→ **Monitoring**
→ **Change / Incident**
→ **Learning**
→ **Revalidation / Reauthorisation**
→ **Workforce**
→ **Audit**
→ **Maturity Assessment**
→ **Gap Prioritisation**
→ **Improvement**
→ **Verification**
→ **Continuous Improvement**

Maturity therefore measures the organisation's ability to sustain and improve the Golden Thread.

---

# 39. Final Maturity Principle

D-AIGAAF treats maturity as a mechanism for understanding and improving organisational capability.

The governing principle is:

> **Maturity should be assessed through demonstrated capability, credible evidence, risk-informed evaluation and independent challenge where appropriate; it should be used to identify and prioritise improvements, but it should never be treated as a substitute for capability-specific evidence, assurance, compliance or explicit operational authorisation.**
