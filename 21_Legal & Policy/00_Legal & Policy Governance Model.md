# 00-Legal and Policy Governance Model

## Purpose

This document establishes the D-AIGAAF governance model for identifying, interpreting, translating, implementing, evidencing, reviewing, and maintaining legal and policy requirements applicable to defence AI capabilities.

The Legal & Policy module provides the authoritative-governance layer connecting external and internal obligations to the D-AIGAAF lifecycle. It is intended to ensure that legal and policy requirements are not treated as isolated compliance statements, but are translated into mission-relevant requirements, controls, evidence, operational conditions, and decision authorities.

## Core Principle

**Legal and policy obligations must be translated into clear, traceable and operationally enforceable governance requirements throughout the AI lifecycle.**

Legal review alone does not establish technical assurance, operational suitability, or authority to employ an AI capability.

The framework therefore preserves the distinction:

**Legal Permissibility ≠ Policy Compliance ≠ Assurance ≠ Operational Readiness ≠ Operational Authorisation**

Similarly:

**Technical Capability ≠ Legal Authority ≠ Operational Authority**

---

## 1. Governance Objective

The Legal & Policy governance model should enable an organisation to answer:

1. What legal and policy requirements apply?
2. Why do they apply?
3. Who is responsible for interpreting them?
4. How are they translated into AI requirements and controls?
5. What evidence demonstrates implementation?
6. What uncertainty or disagreement remains?
7. What operational restrictions follow?
8. Who has authority to accept residual legal or policy risk?
9. What changes require reassessment?
10. How is the legal and policy position preserved through the AI lifecycle?

---

## 2. Legal & Policy Governance Chain

The core governance chain is:

**Authority → Applicability → Interpretation → Obligation → Requirement → Control → Evidence → Assurance → Operational Condition → Authorisation → Monitoring → Change → Reassessment**

This chain should remain traceable for consequential AI capabilities.

---

## 3. Sources of Authority

Legal and policy requirements may originate from multiple levels.

### 3.1 Law

Potential sources include:

- Constitution or foundational law
- Primary legislation
- Subordinate legislation
- Regulations
- Judicial decisions where applicable
- Administrative requirements
- Data protection law
- Cybersecurity law
- Procurement law
- Intellectual property law
- Export controls
- National-security law
- Sector-specific law

### 3.2 International Obligations

Where applicable:

- Treaties
- International humanitarian law
- International human rights law
- International agreements
- Status-of-forces arrangements
- Coalition agreements
- Information-sharing agreements
- Export-control regimes

### 3.3 Defence and Government Policy

Potential sources include:

- National AI policy
- Defence AI policy
- Responsible AI principles
- Defence directives
- Departmental policy
- Service regulations
- Security instructions
- Acquisition policy
- Operational policy
- Information-classification policy
- Records-management policy

### 3.4 Operational Authority

Requirements may also arise from:

- Command directives
- Mission orders
- Rules of engagement
- Operational restrictions
- National caveats
- Coalition conditions
- Theatre-specific requirements

### 3.5 Contracts and Agreements

Relevant requirements may arise from:

- Procurement contracts
- Supplier agreements
- Software licences
- Cloud-service agreements
- Data licences
- Support arrangements
- Memoranda of understanding
- Coalition agreements
- Data-sharing agreements

### 3.6 Standards and Frameworks

Standards and frameworks may influence governance through:

- Formal adoption
- Contractual incorporation
- Policy requirement
- Assurance expectations
- Good practice

A voluntary standard should not automatically be represented as a legal obligation.

---

## 4. Authority Hierarchy

Where multiple sources apply, the organisation should establish the authority and relationship between them.

A typical hierarchy may include:

**Law / Treaty → Regulation → Government Policy → Defence Policy → Organisational Directive → Operational Order → Procedure → Technical Control**

The exact hierarchy depends on jurisdiction and organisational structure.

Where requirements conflict, the conflict should be explicitly identified and referred to competent legal or policy authority.

---

## 5. Governance Roles

### 5.1 Legal Authority

Responsible for:

- Legal interpretation
- Applicability analysis
- Identification of legal uncertainty
- Advice on conflicts of law
- Review of material changes
- Legal implications of incidents

### 5.2 Policy Authority

Responsible for:

- Policy interpretation
- Translating strategic intent into organisational requirements
- Resolving policy ambiguity
- Maintaining policy alignment
- Updating governance requirements

### 5.3 Capability Owner

Responsible for:

- Providing accurate technical and operational information
- Implementing applicable requirements
- Maintaining traceability
- Escalating material changes

### 5.4 Operational Authority

Responsible for ensuring that legal and policy restrictions are reflected in:

- Mission boundaries
- Human authority
- Autonomy limits
- Operational conditions
- Employment decisions

### 5.5 Technical / Security / Data Functions

Responsible for translating relevant requirements into technical and procedural controls.

### 5.6 TEVV and Assurance Functions

Responsible for evaluating whether relevant controls are implemented and supported by sufficient evidence.

### 5.7 Risk Owner

Responsible for decisions involving residual risk within delegated authority.

Legal obligations that cannot lawfully be waived should not be converted into ordinary discretionary risk acceptance.

---

## 6. Legal & Policy Governance Object

For D-AIGAAF, legal and policy governance should be assessed against:

**AI Capability × Mission × Jurisdiction × Environment × Data × Autonomy × Human Authority × Lifecycle Stage**

A change to any of these dimensions may change the legal or policy position.

---

## 7. Requirement Classification

Requirements should be classified to avoid ambiguity.

### Binding Legal Requirement

Mandatory under applicable law.

### Binding Policy Requirement

Mandatory under applicable government, defence, departmental, organisational, or operational policy.

### Contractual Requirement

Mandatory because of an agreement or contract.

### Adopted Standard

A standard made mandatory through policy, contract, procurement, or governance decision.

### Guidance

Authoritative or advisory guidance that informs implementation but is not independently binding.

### Good Practice

A practice used to improve governance or assurance without being represented as a mandatory legal obligation.

---

## 8. Requirement Translation

Legal and policy language is often too abstract to function directly as an operational control.

D-AIGAAF therefore uses the translation chain:

**Source → Obligation → Governance Requirement → Control → Evidence → Assurance → Operational Condition**

Example structure:

| Source | Obligation | Governance Requirement | Control | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

The translation should preserve the meaning of the original obligation while making implementation testable and traceable.

## 11. Legal Uncertainty

AI capabilities may create legal or policy questions for which authoritative interpretation is incomplete, evolving, or context-dependent.

Uncertainty should be explicitly recorded.

Possible classifications:

- Settled
- Reasonably clear
- Contested
- Emerging
- Uncertain
- Requires authoritative determination

Uncertainty should lead to proportionate governance action rather than being hidden through artificial certainty.

Possible actions include:

- Additional legal review
- Operational restriction
- Increased human authority
- Reduced autonomy
- Additional TEVV
- Additional monitoring
- Temporary authorisation
- Escalation
- Suspension

---

## 12. Legal and Policy Constraints

Legal or policy requirements may establish constraints on:

- Mission
- Target or object
- Data
- Geography
- Time
- User
- Human authority
- Autonomy
- Information sharing
- Model access
- Tool access
- External interfaces
- Suppliers
- Cross-border processing
- Retention
- Operational employment

Where a constraint is critical, it should be translated into enforceable technical or procedural controls where practicable.

---

## 13. Human Authority

Legal and policy governance should identify:

- Who may make consequential decisions
- What authority may be delegated
- What authority must remain human
- What decisions require escalation
- What decisions require independent review
- What records are required
- What authority cannot be transferred to AI

An AI system must not acquire legal or operational authority merely because it is technically capable of acting.

---

## 14. Autonomy Governance

Legal and policy review should consider whether changes in autonomy affect:

- Applicable law
- Accountability
- Human decision requirements
- Operational restrictions
- TEVV requirements
- Assurance requirements
- Authorisation level

Increasing autonomy should not occur through configuration drift or technical capability without explicit governance review.

---

## 15. Operational Environment

The legal and policy position may change with:

- Geographic location
- Host nation
- Peace / crisis / armed-conflict context
- Coalition composition
- Mission type
- Population affected
- Data environment
- Classification
- Communications environment
- Rules of engagement
- National caveats

An AI capability authorised in one environment should not automatically be assumed legally or policy-equivalent in another.

---

## 16. Lifecycle Integration

### Mission Definition

Identify applicable legal and policy constraints before requirements are fixed.

### Development / Acquisition

Translate obligations into contractual, technical, security, data, documentation, and evidence requirements.

### TEVV

Test relevant controls and operational restrictions.

### Operational Authorisation

Confirm that material legal and policy conditions have been identified and incorporated.

### Operational Employment

Maintain authorised conditions and record material decisions where required.

### Continuous Assurance

Monitor whether assumptions and requirements remain valid.

### Change

Assess whether changes alter legal or policy applicability.

### Incident

Assess legal, policy, reporting, evidence-preservation, accountability, and notification implications.

### Retirement

Address retention, disposal, contractual, security, archival, and legal-hold requirements.

---

## 17. Legal & Policy Assurance

Legal and policy assurance should examine whether:

- Applicable sources were identified
- Interpretations are authoritative enough for the decision
- Requirements were translated correctly
- Controls were implemented
- Evidence exists
- Operational conditions reflect the requirements
- Uncertainty is visible
- Changes are monitored
- Decisions remain within delegated authority

Legal assurance should not be reduced to the existence of a legal opinion.

---

## 18. Relationship to Operational Authorisation

Operational authorisation should consider legal and policy evidence as one component of the decision.

The following distinctions remain mandatory:

**Lawful ≠ Safe**

**Lawful ≠ Secure**

**Lawful ≠ Effective**

**Lawful ≠ Assured**

**Lawful ≠ Operationally Authorised**

Conversely, technical assurance cannot cure a legally prohibited activity.

---

## 19. Change and Reassessment

Reassessment should be triggered by material changes including:

- New law
- New regulation
- New policy
- New authoritative interpretation
- Mission change
- Jurisdiction change
- Data change
- Supplier change
- New interface
- Autonomy change
- Human-authority change
- Operational-environment change
- Model or system change
- Incident
- Coalition change
- Contract change

A change may be legally material even where no source code or model weights have changed.

---

## 20. Exceptions, Waivers and Risk Acceptance

The governance process should distinguish:

- Legal prohibition
- Mandatory legal obligation
- Policy requirement
- Waivable policy requirement
- Contractual requirement
- Risk-based governance control

The authority to accept operational risk does not automatically include authority to waive law or binding policy.

Any exception or waiver should identify:

- Requirement
- Authority
- Legal basis
- Scope
- Duration
- Conditions
- Compensating controls
- Approving authority
- Review date

---

## 21. Evidence and Traceability

Relevant evidence may include:

- Legal opinions
- Policy interpretations
- Applicability assessments
- Requirement registers
- Contract clauses
- Licences
- Export approvals
- Data agreements
- Operational directives
- Rules of engagement
- Technical-control evidence
- TEVV results
- Authorisation decisions
- Incident records
- Change assessments

The organisation should be able to trace:

**Authority → Requirement → Control → Evidence → Decision**

---

## 22. Review and Governance Cycle

The Legal & Policy governance cycle is:

**Identify → Interpret → Translate → Implement → Evidence → Review → Authorise → Monitor → Update**

Periodic review should assess:

- Changes in law
- Changes in policy
- Changes in technology
- Changes in operational use
- New incidents
- New legal interpretations
- New international obligations
- Supplier and contractual changes
- Lessons from assurance and audit

---

## 23. Minimum Governance Requirements

For consequential AI capabilities, the organisation should at minimum establish:

1. Applicable legal and policy sources.
2. Responsible legal and policy authorities.
3. Applicability assessment.
4. Requirement classification.
5. Requirement-to-control traceability.
6. Legal and policy decision records.
7. Explicit treatment of uncertainty.
8. Operational restrictions.
9. Human-authority implications.
10. Autonomy implications.
11. Evidence requirements.
12. Change triggers.
13. Review mechanisms.
14. Relationship to operational authorisation.
15. Records and retention requirements.

---

## 24. Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

1. Treating legal review as a final-stage approval.
2. Treating all AI guidance as law.
3. Assuming defence or national-security use eliminates legal obligations.
4. Treating legal permissibility as operational authorisation.
5. Treating technical assurance as proof of legality.
6. Hiding legal uncertainty.
7. Allowing suppliers to define the organisation's legal position.
8. Using generic compliance statements without requirement-level traceability.
9. Failing to reassess after mission or jurisdiction changes.
10. Allowing technical capability to expand authority.
11. Treating risk acceptance as authority to waive law.
12. Creating legal governance disconnected from operational decisions.

---

## 25. Governance Test

For every consequential AI capability, governance authorities should be able to answer:

> **What legal and policy authorities govern this capability, why do they apply, how have their obligations been translated into controls and operational conditions, what evidence supports implementation, what uncertainty remains, who holds decision authority, and what changes would require reassessment?**

If these questions cannot be answered, the legal and policy governance position is incomplete.

## Final Principle

**Legal and policy governance should not sit beside the AI lifecycle as a separate compliance activity. It should shape the requirements, controls, evidence, authority, operational boundaries, change decisions, and accountability through which defence AI is governed from mission conception to retirement.**
