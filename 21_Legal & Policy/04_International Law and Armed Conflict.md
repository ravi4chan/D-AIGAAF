# 04-International Law and Armed Conflict

## Purpose

This document establishes the D-AIGAAF governance approach for integrating applicable international law, including international humanitarian law (IHL) and other rules relevant to armed conflict, into the lifecycle and operational governance of defence AI capabilities.

It is a governance architecture, not a substitute for case-specific legal advice. Its purpose is to ensure that applicable legal obligations, legal reviews, operational constraints, human authority, technical controls, TEVV evidence, and authorisation decisions remain connected.

## Core Principle

**The use of AI does not displace the legal obligations governing military operations. AI-supported decisions and actions must remain subject to applicable law and accountable human authority.**

Technical capability, model confidence, automation, or autonomy does not independently establish legal authority.

## Governance Chain

**Applicable Law → Legal Interpretation → Mission Requirement → Operational Constraint → Human Authority → Technical / Procedural Control → TEVV → Evidence → Legal Review → Operational Authorisation → Employment → Monitoring → Reassessment**

---

## 1. Scope

This governance model may apply to AI capabilities supporting:

- Intelligence
- Surveillance
- Reconnaissance
- Decision support
- Target-related processes
- Force protection
- Navigation
- Logistics
- Cyber operations
- Information operations
- Command and control
- Autonomous or semi-autonomous functions
- Uncrewed systems
- Defensive systems
- Mission planning

The applicable legal analysis will depend on the specific capability, mission, context, jurisdiction, actor, and intended use.

---

## 2. Applicable Bodies of International Law

Depending on context, relevant bodies may include:

- International humanitarian law
- International human rights law
- International law governing the use of force
- Treaty obligations
- Customary international law
- Weapons-related treaties
- Neutrality law
- International agreements
- Status-of-forces arrangements
- Coalition agreements

Applicability should be determined by competent legal authority.

---

## 3. Conflict Classification and Context

Legal requirements may depend on the nature of the operational context.

Record:

- Operational context
- Applicable legal regime
- Conflict classification where relevant
- Parties
- Geographic scope
- Relevant agreements
- Legal assumptions
- Uncertainties
- Reviewing authority

AI systems should not independently determine the applicable legal regime unless their role is explicitly limited to decision support and competent human authority retains the legal determination.

---

## 4. Distinction

Where IHL applies, governance should support the ability of responsible humans to distinguish lawful military objectives from civilians and civilian objects as required by applicable law.

AI governance should therefore address:

- Data relevance
- Sensor limitations
- Classification uncertainty
- False positives
- False negatives
- Context loss
- Temporal validity
- Object identification
- Behavioural inference
- Adversarial deception
- Human review

AI classification output should not be treated as equivalent to a legal determination.

---

## 5. Proportionality

Where applicable, responsible decision-makers must be able to make the legally required assessment concerning expected incidental harm and anticipated military advantage.

AI may support information processing, estimation, modelling, or decision support, but governance should ensure that:

- Assumptions are visible
- Uncertainty is communicated
- Data limitations are known
- Human authority is identifiable
- Relevant contextual information is available
- Decision records can be reconstructed where required

A model score should not be treated as a substitute for the legally required human judgment.

---

## 6. Precautions

Governance should support feasible precautions required by applicable law.

AI-related controls may include:

- Verification of relevant information
- Alternative-course analysis
- Confidence and uncertainty presentation
- Human review
- Updated data
- Abort / suspend mechanisms
- Target or object restrictions
- Geographic restrictions
- Timing restrictions
- Tool restrictions
- Reassessment after material change

---

## 7. Military Necessity and Humanity

Where applicable, legal review should consider the relationship between legitimate military purpose and applicable humanitarian constraints.

D-AIGAAF should ensure that mission optimisation does not become the sole governing objective where other legal constraints apply.

---

## 8. Weapons Review

Where a state has applicable obligations or policy for legal review of new weapons, means, or methods of warfare, AI-enabled capabilities should be assessed to determine whether such review is required.

The review process should establish:

- System under review
- Intended use
- Technical characteristics
- Autonomy
- Human control
- Expected effects
- Failure modes
- Operational environment
- Constraints
- Relevant legal rules
- Evidence
- Review conclusion
- Conditions or limitations

A prior legal review may require reassessment after material changes.

---

## 9. AI as Component Versus System

Legal analysis should establish whether AI is:

- A supporting analytical component
- A decision-support component
- A navigation or control component
- A targeting-related component
- An autonomous function
- Part of a broader weapon or mission system

The legal significance of AI depends on its actual function and effects, not merely on the label applied to the technology.

---

## 10. Human Authority and Legal Judgment

For consequential applications, identify:

- Who holds legal decision authority
- Who holds operational authority
- What information the person receives
- Whether uncertainty is visible
- Whether intervention is possible
- Whether sufficient time exists
- Whether escalation is available
- Whether accountability is traceable

**Nominal human presence is not sufficient if the person lacks the authority, information, competence, time, or ability to intervene meaningfully.**

---

## 11. Autonomy

Legal and policy review should consider whether autonomy affects:

- Predictability
- Human control
- Geographic scope
- Temporal scope
- Target or object scope
- Ability to intervene
- Ability to abort
- Accountability
- Operational constraints
- Weapons-review conclusions

Increasing autonomy may require additional legal review, TEVV, assurance, and reauthorisation.

---

## 12. Predictability and Reliability

For legally consequential functions, assess:

- Expected behaviour
- Known failure modes
- Environmental sensitivity
- Distribution shift
- Adversarial manipulation
- Sensor degradation
- Communications loss
- Model uncertainty
- Software failure
- Emergent behaviour
- Tool interaction

Unpredictability that affects legally relevant behaviour should be treated as a governance and authorisation concern.

---

## 13. Data and Evidence

Legal assessments may depend on data concerning:

- Objects
- Persons
- Locations
- Behaviour
- Environment
- Timing
- Intelligence
- Sensor observations

Governance should assess:

- Provenance
- Reliability
- Currency
- Integrity
- Representativeness
- Uncertainty
- Contradiction
- Adversarial manipulation

---

## 14. Operational Environment

Legal and operational assumptions should be assessed against:

- Urban environment
- Civilian presence
- Protected objects
- Communications conditions
- Sensor visibility
- Weather
- Electromagnetic conditions
- Adversarial deception
- Coalition operations
- Cross-border conditions
- Rapidly changing battlefield conditions

A capability validated in one environment should not automatically be assumed suitable for another.

---

## 15. Target-Related Decision Support

Where AI contributes to target-related processes, governance should clearly identify:

- AI function
- Human function
- Source data
- Uncertainty
- Verification requirements
- Decision authority
- Approval chain
- Prohibited automated actions
- Evidence and logs
- Abort / suspend mechanisms

AI output should be treated according to its validated role, not as inherently authoritative.

---

## 16. Protected Persons and Objects

Where relevant, governance should account for legal protections applicable to persons or objects.

Technical controls may support:

- Exclusion lists
- Protected-area data
- Geospatial restrictions
- Object classification
- Human verification
- Alerts
- Escalation

Such technical controls support, but do not replace, applicable legal judgment.

---

## 17. Surrender, Incapacitation and Contextual Judgments

Some legally relevant determinations depend heavily on context, behaviour, intent, status, or rapidly changing circumstances.

Where AI cannot reliably establish such context, its role should be appropriately constrained.

Governance should identify:

- What AI may infer
- What AI cannot determine reliably
- What requires human judgment
- What uncertainty must be displayed
- What actions are prohibited without human confirmation

---

## 18. Degraded and Disconnected Operations

Define lawful and authorised behaviour when:

- Human supervision is lost
- Communications fail
- Sensor confidence falls
- Positioning becomes unreliable
- Data becomes stale
- Coalition information disappears
- Monitoring fails

Loss of communication should not automatically expand operational or legal authority.

---

## 19. Fail-Safe and Abort

For relevant systems, establish:

- Abort mechanism
- Suspend mechanism
- Human intervention
- Safe state
- Geographic containment
- Temporal containment
- Action containment
- Recovery conditions

A safe state must be mission- and context-specific.

---

## 20. Rules of Engagement and Operational Orders

Where applicable, AI employment must remain consistent with current:

- Rules of engagement
- Operational orders
- Command directives
- National caveats
- Mission-specific restrictions

These should be configuration-controlled and traceable to the operational authorisation.

---

## 21. Coalition Operations

Coalition use may create differences in:

- Legal interpretation
- National policy
- Rules of engagement
- Data-sharing authority
- Targeting procedures
- Human-authority requirements
- AI policy
- Technical standards

Coalition interoperability must not silently erase national legal or policy constraints.

---

## 22. Legal Review Evidence

Evidence may include:

- Legal-review record
- Weapons-review record
- System description
- Intended-use statement
- Operational scenarios
- Autonomy assessment
- Human-control assessment
- TEVV results
- Failure-mode analysis
- Security assessment
- Operational constraints
- Configuration baseline
- Authorisation record

---

## 23. Change and Re-Review

Legal reassessment should be considered after material changes to:

- Intended use
- Mission
- Autonomy
- Model
- Data
- Sensors
- Effectors
- Tools
- Operational environment
- Human authority
- Geographic scope
- Supplier
- Rules of engagement
- System integration

A software version number alone is insufficient to determine whether legal re-review is required.

---

## 24. Incident Review

Where an incident has potential legal significance:

- Preserve evidence
- Record system state
- Preserve configuration
- Identify human decisions
- Preserve AI outputs
- Identify applicable authority
- Escalate to appropriate legal and command authorities
- Avoid premature causal conclusions
- Distinguish fact, inference, and uncertainty

---

## 25. Operational Authorisation

Legal review is one input to operational authorisation.

Authorisation should confirm that relevant legal conditions and restrictions are reflected in the authorised operational envelope.

The D-AIGAAF authorisation object remains:

**AI Capability × Mission × Environment × Autonomy × Human Authority**

---

## 26. Minimum Governance Record

For legally consequential defence AI, record at minimum:

- Capability
- Intended use
- Mission
- Operational context
- Applicable legal framework
- Legal-review authority
- Human decision authority
- Autonomy
- Material legal constraints
- TEVV evidence
- Operational limitations
- Rules of engagement / directives where applicable
- Configuration
- Authorisation
- Review triggers

---

## 27. Governance Test

Before operational authorisation, ask:

> **Does the governance system provide responsible human decision-makers with the authority, information, competence, time, controls, evidence, and intervention mechanisms necessary to employ this AI capability consistently with applicable international law and mission-specific legal constraints?**

If not, the capability should not be assumed legally or operationally ready.

---

## 28. Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

1. Treating AI output as a legal determination.
2. Assuming automation transfers legal responsibility.
3. Treating a legal review as permanent despite material system changes.
4. Treating high model confidence as legal certainty.
5. Assuming technical classification equals legal status.
6. Hiding uncertainty from decision-makers.
7. Allowing loss of communications to expand authority automatically.
8. Treating coalition interoperability as automatic legal equivalence.
9. Relying on generic ethical principles instead of applicable legal analysis.
10. Treating technical assurance as proof of legal compliance.

---

## D-AIGAAF Integration

International-law governance integrates with:

**Mission → Applicable Law → Legal Constraints → Requirements → Human Authority → Technical / Procedural Controls → TEVV → Evidence → Assurance → Operational Authorisation → Employment → Monitoring → Incident / Change → Legal Reassessment → Reauthorisation**

It connects particularly to Mission & Use Case, Risk & Autonomy, Human Authority, TEVV, Operational Environment, Operational Authorisation, Operational Employment, Incident & Fail-Safe, Change & Reauthorisation, Interoperability & Coalition, and Architecture & Technical Controls.

---

## Final Principle

**AI may support military judgment, but it does not replace the legal obligations or human accountability governing the use of military power. D-AIGAAF must therefore ensure that applicable international law is translated into operationally meaningful constraints, controls, evidence, and human decision authority throughout the AI lifecycle.**
